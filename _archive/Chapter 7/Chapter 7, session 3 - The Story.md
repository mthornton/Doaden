## The Story

### I

They decided to wait.

It was not a brave decision and nobody pretended otherwise. The armory door was oak and damp at the bottom and Wolfram was fairly sure he could take it off its hinges, and he was entirely sure that the sound of him doing it would carry across that yard like a dropped bell. So they stood where they had been put, at the edge of a crowd of men who had been drinking since the fence went up, and they waited for somebody to start a killing.

The yard was full. Every guard in the compound, every slave they had dragged out to watch, Ysabel's household clustered close and trying not to look at anything. In the middle of it a ring of sharpened timber, finished at last, and inside the ring a gnome in rags with a brand on his shoulder and nothing in his hands.

Old Cobb was still lying in the mud by the Hall steps where Vaskar had thrown him. Nobody had moved him. Nobody was going to.

Vaskar raised his voice and called for the fight to begin.

### II

Luthien spoke first, and what he spoke was not a word anyone in that yard understood.

It was a small working, and a strange one to spend in the circumstances — not a weapon, not a wall. A kind of turning-aside. He put it on the gnome, and the air around the little man went briefly thick, the way air goes over a hot road, and then it did not look like anything at all.

The Juggernaut came off his mark like a landslide.

What happened next, the whole compound saw, and not one man in it understood it.

The giant covered half the distance to the gnome and then stopped. Stopped the way a cart stops when a wheel comes off — all the momentum still in him and nowhere for it to go. He stood in the middle of the ring with his hands open and his masked head turning, and the thing he had been told to kill was eighteen inches from his knee and he could not make himself want it.

So he wanted something else instead.

Mott bent, took hold of one of the barricade sections — a slab of lashed, sharpened logs as long as a man is tall — and tore it out of the ground like a weed. Then he turned, and threw it.

It went into the knot of men standing outside the doors of the Deeps: Ironwake's own, the two who had walked him out, the professionals. It caught both of them across the chest and carried them back into the stone and left them sitting in the mud, bleeding, wearing the astonished expression of men to whom the evening had just been explained.

There was a hole in the ring where the barricade had been.

Gimble was through it before the first man shouted.

### III

Everything happened in the same breath, and the order of it only exists because somebody wrote it down afterward.

JonLief threw a shimmer of light into the air in front of Durgan — a cheap trick, a flicker to pull a man's eyes the wrong way. The dwarf did not so much as blink. He had spent nineteen years watching one thing and he was watching it now.

And Wolfram ran at the armory door and put his shoulder through it.

It did not creak and it did not splinter in the middle. It came off its hinges whole, in one piece, and went flat on the floor inside with a crash that ended every conversation in that compound.

The slaves understood it instantly. Nobody told them. Nobody had to. Thirty-odd people who had spent the evening being beaten for the entertainment of drunks turned, as one animal, and ran for the open door of the armory.

### IV

The guards nearest them tried to restore order the only way they knew.

A club came down on the back of a man's skull and he went into the mud and did not get up. Vaskar came forward off the Hall steps with two of his own behind him, shouting — *the armory, get them off the armory* — and for about four seconds it looked as though twenty armed men might still own that yard.

Then Durgan Half-Ear made the only decision of his life that mattered.

He had been handling that giant for nineteen years. He had kept him fed and kept him calm and kept him from doing exactly this, every day, in every town Ironwake sent them to, and he had done it because the alternative was a man he loved being put down like a dog. And he looked at a yard where every guard in the place had its back turned, and a hole in the fence, and a road north, and he understood that there would not be another night like this one.

He said: *Mott. Kill the guards.*

The giant reached over the barricade and picked two men up off the ground, one in each hand, the way a child lifts cats. Then he brought them down on the spikes of the fence he had built that afternoon.

### V

Anselm Vogt reached the armory with three others and started handing weapons out through the door.

Wolfram stood in front of it and did not let anyone past. A guard came at him and he opened the man's arm to the bone and the guard reeled off into the crowd, and then there was no crowd to reel into, because the crowd had turned.

This is the part that nobody in Solteres will ever believe, and it is also what actually happened: they were mostly still unarmed. Thirty people with bare hands and fence timber against men in chain shirts with spears, and it was not close. One of them went onto a spearpoint and was carried along on it, and the man holding the spear was pulled down by six others and did not come back up.

Virgil raised his hand and brought a column of pale light down out of nothing onto a cluster of guards by the pens, and they staggered inside it, and he walked it slowly across the yard like a man moving a lantern, and wherever it went men fell out of the fight.

JonLief tried a working on a guard and missed him entirely. He made a small disgusted sound, drew his sword instead, and started around the outside of the ring to get at the men on the far side, which was the sort of thing he had been doing all chapter.

Mott took hold of another one and pulled his head off.

### VI

Up on the balcony of the Deeps — the good seats, the place you watched the fight from if you mattered — a dozen guards came off the rail at once and went for the stairs.

It would take them half a minute. Three flights, a landing, and the front door. Half a minute is a very long time in a yard like that one.

Wolfram cut the wounded guard's head from his shoulders, and the swing carried him round, and he put the edge of his shield into the face of the next man on the follow-through. The slaves, some of them armed now, closed over the last of the guards by the armory and killed every one of them.

Which was when Vaskar decided the night was lost.

He crossed the yard at a run, took Lady Ysabel Rochefort by the arm, and dragged her into his house.

### VII

Mott tore up another section of the fence and brought it down and hit nothing at all. Then he went down onto his knees in the mud with his chest heaving, and he stayed there, because he was finished. Four minutes of that will finish anything.

The balcony guards came out of the front door of the Deeps and found him kneeling.

They surrounded him and began to beat him. A dozen men with clubs and spear-butts on a thing that could not stand up, and they were not in a hurry, because he had just killed five of them and they wanted him to know about it.

Durgan threw himself on top of him.

He did not fight. There was nothing in him to fight with — a stout dwarf in a leather apron on top of a mountain, with his arms spread over as much of it as he could cover, taking every blow that would otherwise have landed. He had spent nineteen years keeping that giant from being killed, and when it came to it this was the whole of the method, and he used it.

Gimble Beren was not in the yard for that.

He had gone the other way — to the small prison, where the ones who could not fight had been left locked in the dark to listen. He went down the row and opened every door, and the people who came out of those cells were old, or sick, or too weak to lift a club, and they came out anyway.

The first thing Dave's character ever did in this campaign was let somebody else out of a cell.

### VIII

In the yard, the slaves began tearing down the barricade.

It was between them and the dwarf and the thing he was lying on, and it had been built that afternoon by men who were now dead, and it came apart faster than it had gone up. Wolfram got his hands into it. Luthien got his hands into it. Thirty people pulled a wall down with their fingers.

When it came down they went over the top of it and killed every guard still standing in Bramblefen.

By the time it was quiet there were two men alive in that compound who had worn Vaskar's colours at sundown. Vaskar, and one guard, both of them inside the Hall, and Ysabel in there with them.

### IX

Wolfram stood at the bottom of the steps with blood drying on his shield and shouted up at the door.

"Vaskar. If you want to live you need to come out with Ysabel. She had better be unharmed."

He came out. Of course he came out. He was never a man who was going to die on principle — he was a manager, and he had been doing arithmetic since the moment the armory door hit the floor, and he had arrived at the only number available to him.

He offered a trade, and he offered it the way he would have offered a price on a woman: plainly, without heat, as though they were both professionals and this was Tuesday. The lady, unhurt. And the ledger — the real one, the current one, the book that recorded what Bramblefen had actually been doing these last six weeks. It was hidden. He would tell them where.

And then the last guard alive at Bramblefen put his hand up.

"I know where it is," he said. "I'll gladly tell you."

Vaskar did not even look at him.

The negotiation went on a while after that, and Wolfram ran it, and when it was done Ysabel came down the steps on her own feet and the book changed hands and Vaskar was given the gate.

On his way across the yard he said the only true thing he said all night. Ironwake would have him killed for this. For letting the fen fall, and for the slaves — not the men, the *slaves*, the loss on the books. He said it without self-pity, as a point of information, in the tone of a man reading out a figure.

Then he walked.

Luthien stopped him in the gateway and went through his bag. Rations, and a great deal of coin.

Luthien took all of it.

Wolfram reached into the pile, took out a small handful, and dropped it back in Vaskar's bag. Then he put a hand between the slaver's shoulder blades and pushed him out the gate.

The rest of the coin he distributed among thirty freed slaves, in the mud, by hand, while Luthien watched him do it with the expression of a man being disembowelled.

### X

Afterward, there was food.

Somebody had the idea of raiding the kitchens and the idea was unanimously popular. Thirty people who had been fed on whatever Bramblefen fed them sat in the yard of the place that had owned them and ate its stores, and for a while the loudest sound in that compound was chewing.

The party went into the Deeps while they ate.

They had all felt it on the way in and none of them had said much about it — the thing that came off that building, the pressure of three centuries of people kept in it. It was less than St. Pyotr's. It was recognisably the same.

They found a barrel.

Standard cooperage, iron-hooped, pitched at the seams, heavier than it had any right to be. And when they opened it, it was blood — fresh, and a long way from any animal, and every one of them had seen it before. On a beach on Dol Island, going aboard a ship. In a stone circle in a chapel at St. Pyotr's with black fog coming off it.

Virgil took a sample. The rest of it ran out through the bottom of the barrel and into the floor of the Deeps and was gone.

Nobody in that building said the obvious thing out loud, which is that there had been more than one barrel on that beach, and that somebody had been shipping them for a long time, and that this one had come *here*.

### XI

The freed began to get restless, the way people do when the fighting is over and nobody has told them what happens next.

So Luthien played.

He had been doing this since the first night of this campaign, in worse rooms than this, to worse audiences. But something has been happening to his music for a while now, and the people closest to him have started to notice it, and so has he, and none of them have said it. Every time he plays he is better than he was the time before, by more than practice accounts for.

That night in that yard he played better than he has ever played in his life.

And while he played, at the edge of his vision, there was a woman.

She was glowing — faintly, the way a window glows at dusk from outside — and she had too many arms. Four of them. Then six. It was difficult to be certain, because every time he turned his head to look straight at her there was nothing there at all, only thirty exhausted people sitting in the mud listening to a song.

When the last note went, she went with it.

He has not worked out who she was. He will.

### XII

Then Anselm Vogt came and offered the party his service, and the service of every one of them, because almost none of them had anywhere to go.

That was the part nobody had planned for. Thirty people. No food, no carts to spare, no road that led anywhere good. The party said so, carefully, and started trying to find a kind way to say *we cannot feed you* —

— and Anselm stopped them, because he had heard where they were going.

Solteres.

They could not go to Solteres. Half of them were not full-blooded human, and in that city that is not a quality, it is a legal status, and thirty freed slaves walking up the road to Solteres would be back in a pen inside a week with the paperwork done properly. They had to go the other way. Away from the city, and fast.

So they were told to go to **Ald's Star**, and to say that Wolfram sent them.

### XIII

Ysabel got her book.

She asked for both, naturally, and was told no, and took the one with reasonably good grace — Cobb's ledger, thirty-eight years of Bramblefen's business in a dead clerk's hand. She has a plan for it, and she did not trouble to hide it: there are names in that book attached to houses in Solteres that would pay a great deal not to see them read aloud, and houses like that can lean on a man like Ironwake, and a man like Ironwake can be persuaded to forget about a trial by combat.

She was in a good mood. She had come to Bramblefen to buy seamstresses, been taken hostage, and come out of it holding a weapon.

The party kept the working ledger. It has a name in it.

### XIV

And then, when the eating was done and the night had gone properly cold and it genuinely seemed that the day was finished with them, the head of a hammer came through the front gate.

One blow. The timber went in like a biscuit. Then a boot, and the gate swung wide, and two men walked into the yard in long cloaks of deep violet.

A very large older man, and a thin young one beside him.

The big one introduced himself as **Baltus**. He said he was a Shepherd, and that he had been sent to find them, and nobody in that yard needed to be told what the colour meant.

They told him everything.

They had no particular reason to and there was every reason not to — nineteen dead men, a slaver let go, a compound taken, a noblewoman in their company and a stolen book in their pack. They told him anyway, and he listened to all of it, standing in the mud with silver chiming softly on his coat, and when they finished he looked around that yard at thirty living people eating stolen bread.

He was *pleased.* Genuinely, visibly, uncomplicatedly pleased. He has thirty-one small silver lambs sewn onto that cloak and not one mark anywhere on him for the ones he brought home, and he stood in Bramblefen and looked at a night that had gone well, and it was plain to everyone watching that this does not happen to him often.

They laid out the plan. Use the ledger. Find where the women were taken, or find where that barrel was going. But Rowan first.

Baltus agreed with all of it. And he told them something about themselves that nobody in the Church had ever bothered to tell them: that a Penitent has the latitude to act beyond his orders when the chance arises to serve the will of the Emperor. They had been walking around for two and a half months assuming every step off the road was a crime.

### XV

Virgil said he was not going to Solteres.

He said it without apology, which is how he says everything. The cultists were a threat and he had some concern for the boy, and neither of those was the thing keeping him awake. The thing keeping him awake was growing out of a scorched circle in a marsh sixty yards outside that wall, with small green nodules in rows on the underside of its leaves, and he had a sample of it in his pack, and he had come to a conclusion about it.

The Green was going to be a pandemic. He was going to find out what caused it and he was going to find a cure.

He was going to Master Thaddeus.

Baltus agreed that this was the right thing to do.

He also made it fairly clear, in the mildest possible terms, that he thought this party was out of its depth and had not always chosen the way the Church's teaching would have chosen. So he was leaving his apprentice with them.

Then he went back out through the gate he had broken, and the party bedded down for the night inside the walls of Bramblefen, in a compound full of freed people and bodies, with a barrel's worth of a goddess's blood soaking into the floor of the Deeps beneath them.
