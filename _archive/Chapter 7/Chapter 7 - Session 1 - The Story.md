# Chapter Seven: The Chained Coast
## Part One — The Road to Bramblefen

*Session 1, played 16 August 2026. The story of one evening, one morning, and the walk that followed.*

---

### I.

By the last week of the road they had stopped being two groups sharing a fire.

It had happened without anyone deciding it. **Symon Reeve** knew which of the horses would bite and had opinions about all of them. **Cael Ordway**, who was eighteen and desperate not to look eighteen, had taken to setting out the party's bowls before his own. **Mina Dorn** said perhaps forty words a day and thirty of them were useful. **Perrin Aske**, who had run Lady Ysabel's father's household before he ran hers, stood a little behind her shoulder at all times, in a way that had clearly been trained into him so long ago that he no longer knew he was doing it.

And **Dov Brandt** had begun following Wolfram around.

Not obviously. He asked about footwork. He asked how you set a shield when a thing bigger than you is coming, and he listened to the answer as though it were going to be on a test. He had carried a spear in a town militia once, some years ago, and he mentioned it twice, and both times he was careful to say it had not been much of a militia.

Wolfram, who had not been looked at that way by anyone since Westfield, answered every question seriously.

That was where they were on the last night: a fire, a cart, eleven people, and Solteres two days off.

---

### II.

Ysabel had grown quieter each day as the smoke on the horizon got closer, and on the last night she gave up being quiet.

"I beg you to reconsider."

She was not performing. That was the difficulty with her; she never was.

"When I arrive at that trial without a champion, Lord Ironwake will put his own forward, and I will have to stand against him. His champion is not a normal man. He is easily twice the size of the largest man I have ever seen."

Wolfram looked up from the fire.

"Twice the size of *me*?"

"No." Ysabel considered him for a moment, quite honestly, with her head tilted. "You are not the largest man I have ever seen. He is perhaps three times your size."

Nobody said anything for a while after that.

They talked it round anyway, because it deserved talking round. Luthien pointed out — reasonably, and without much heat — that three of the four of them had no business in an arena, and that the fourth would be walking into it against something three times his size. JonLief asked whether magic was permitted in a trial by combat. It was, Ysabel said. It changed nothing.

They asked her what the thing actually *was*, and she told them, and it was worse than they expected.

Trials by combat in Solteres are held in the coliseum, on festival days. The whole day is given over to entertainment — plays, acrobats, animal acts, a dozen other things — and the trial is the last event on the card. It is guaranteed to be brutal and it is guaranteed to end in a death, which is precisely why it is scheduled last. The crowds like it.

"So you would be the finale," said Luthien.

"Yes."

"After the acrobats."

"Yes."

---

### III.

She told them the rest of it that night as well, and she told it plainly, which did not help her.

She had begun as an importer of fine cloth — exotic fabrics out of far-off places, sold on to the dressmakers of Solteres. Some of those places it is not legal to import from. So she had the goods smuggled, and that is how she first met Lord Leofard Ironwake, who controls what comes into that city and what leaves it, and who takes his cut of everything, and who takes rather more of the illegal things.

She paid his taxes for a while. Then she decided she was clever enough not to.

He found out inside a month. He seized an entire shipment and sent word that a second discourtesy would end with her dead in a way people would talk about.

So she changed the shape of the business. She built a mill and wove her own cloth from raw material she brought in herself, worked by slaves she bought at a holdfast in the fens called **Bramblefen**. Her margins doubled. Then she opened a second house making finished dresses, and the dresses were better than anything else in the city, and within a season every noblewoman and every merchant's wife in Solteres wanted one and would pay any price asked. She asked high prices. She went from wealthy to one of the wealthiest women in the city.

And then, being that wealthy, she decided she was finally too big for Ironwake, and she smuggled a shipment past him a second time.

He had her niece poisoned, and arranged the evidence so that Ysabel would hang for it.

She finished the account and looked at them and waited.

---

### IV.

It was hardest for Wolfram, and everyone at that fire knew it, including Ysabel.

He is a protector of the weak. It is not a thing he decided to be; it is the shape he comes in, and refusing a frightened woman who has asked four times goes against the whole of him.

But he is also a man under orders, and the Church did not send him across a continent to die in a coliseum for a merchant he has known for three weeks.

Luthien's decision cost him nothing at all. He said so.

JonLief and Virgil were practical about it in the same breath and for the same two reasons: they had orders, and none of them would survive the fight.

So the answer stayed no, for the fifth time, and the fire burned down, and it was agreed that they would see her as far as the gates of Solteres and no further. After that they would find the Church's house in the city and report in, and stop being men nobody had heard from in two and a half months.

That was the plan when they went to their bedrolls.

It lasted until dawn.

---

### V.

They woke to shouting.

One of Ysabel's men — the one who had drawn the last watch — came to the ashes of the fire dragging a rope, and on the end of it was a woman in rags with both fists still full of last night's supper.

"Caught this one with her hands in the pot. Been at the leavings like a dog."

She went down on her knees before anyone asked her to.

"Please. Please don't hurt me. It's only what you left, I swear it — I waited till you were all abed, I never came near you. I only wanted what was going in the dirt anyway." She was shaking. "I swear it to the Emperor. It's been days with nothing but berries."

In the grey light they could see her properly. Middle-aged. Filthy past the point where filth is a condition and into where it is a covering. And under it, unmistakably, the soft mild markings of Tabaxi about the nose and the ears — half, and not enough of it to be one thing or the other.

Ysabel looked at her for about a second and a half.

"Don't trouble yourselves. It's a slave, and a runaway, and nothing it says will be worth the hearing." She turned to her steward without raising her voice. "Perrin, keep it tied. We'll take it into Solteres with us and hand it back to one of Lord Ironwake's men. It won't undo what's between us, but he may remember the courtesy."

*It.* Twice, in the space of a breath, without any particular malice, the way you would talk about a lost dog with a collar on.

JonLief crouched down instead, so that his eyes were level with hers, and asked her where she had come from.

Her name was **Erwana**.

She had come from Bramblefen.

---

### VI.

The story came out slowly and in the wrong order, because she was starving and roped and had learned across thirty years that volunteering anything is how you get hurt.

There is an old man at Bramblefen. **Old Cobb.** He is the only permanent slave in the place — the bookkeeper, who has recorded every purchase and every sale there for longer than anyone can remember. He told her about a drainage grate at the back of the pens, and the smuggler's channel underneath it that runs beneath the wall and out into the fen.

And he did not send her out empty-handed.

For years Cobb had been quietly keeping a **second set of books** — a true copy of the sales that the men who own that place would most prefer forgotten. He gave it to her the night she ran and asked her to get it out of the fen, and told her to go as far from Solteres as her legs would take her and then find a Church, any Church, and put the book in their hands.

Not the Church in Solteres. That one, he said, looks the other way about slavery and has done for years. But some other city, some other bishop. He believed the Church was good underneath. He believed somebody in it would do something.

She did not know what. She has never read a word of it. She cannot read.

She offered it to them there, on her knees on the wet ground: food, some coin, and let her run, and the book was theirs.

They agreed.

---

### VII.

JonLief opened it himself.

It ran back years. Names, prices, buyers, dates, in a small precise clerk's hand that got shakier as the pages went on. The last entry was about **six weeks** old, and then the writing simply stopped, mid-page, as though the man holding the pen had heard something in the corridor.

Two months back, he found this:

*An elf woman and her son, brought in to Bramblefen.*

*Elizabeth. Rowan.*

The ages were right.

He asked Erwana whether she knew anything, and she said no — and then stopped, and looked at the pendant hanging at his throat, and went very still.

"I knew that."

She said it twice.

"I knew that. She had one. The same. She was in a cell near mine and she was so hot with the fever you could feel it across the straw, and she coughed, all night, every night, and there was a boy with her and she would not let go of him." She looked up. "The guards came one day and took them both out. I don't know where. That's all. That's all I know, I swear it."

---

### VIII.

There was more, and she gave it without being pressed, once she understood they wanted it.

All the women in that holdfast were sold. All of them, in one lot, not long after the guards took the elf woman and the boy away.

All except her.

The guards had laughed about that where she could hear. She smelled so bad, they said, that even filth like *those* wouldn't take her.

The buyers were a small group of men, and she had never seen anything like them. They kept their hoods up. What she could see of them — hands, wrists, the sides of their faces — was covered in scars, old ones and new ones layered over each other, more than any accident or any beating accounts for.

They paid, and they took the women, and Erwana never heard another word about any of them.

She does not know whether the elf woman was part of that sale.

Nobody around that fire said anything for a moment. Three of them had stood on a beach on Dol Island and watched a boat pull away with barrels in it, and none of them said that out loud either.

---

### IX.

Through all of it, Ysabel kept putting her hand out.

She wanted the book. She saw what it was inside about four seconds, which is roughly four seconds faster than anyone else at that fire: the names of politicians, noblemen and merchants who had bought people. Not that anyone in Solteres greatly minds a man buying people. But a *second* set of books means somebody was hiding something, and men do not go to the trouble of hiding a thing unless they would be damaged by its coming out. With that ledger she could squeeze every name in it, and every name in it could squeeze Ironwake, and the charges against her might quietly evaporate.

And in between reaching for it she kept talking about Erwana as though Erwana were furniture.

A slave is ignorant. A slave is not to be trusted. Subhumans are below humans and are suited to nothing better than serving those above them — and slavery does a genuine good for humans too, when applied to criminals, since it gives a man a way to pay what he owes.

"Dov is mine for three more years," she said, by way of illustration.

And she gave them his history, briefly, the way you would describe a horse's bloodline. Gambling debts. A robbery attempted on the Solteres road and very badly attempted. His family's property sold to make restitution, and then five years — which he had discharged, lawfully and by his own choice, by offering himself for sale. She had bought him. He had three years left. He would walk away clear at the end of it, she pointed out, which is more than a cell would have given him.

Nobody made a scene about it.

That is worth writing down accurately: **nobody made a scene about it.** Wolfram did not say anything to her. Nobody stood up. It was early and cold and there was a great deal else happening.

But that was the moment the arithmetic finished itself, quietly, in four heads at once.

Dov Brandt was owned, for a fixed term, on paper, for a crime, by a woman being perfectly reasonable about it over breakfast.

And each of them owed the Church of Azerai five hundred years.

Dov had three left.

---

### X.

The plan changed inside a minute of JonLief reading that entry. He was going to Bramblefen. If Elizabeth and Rowan were still there he was taking them out, and if they were not he was finding out where they had gone.

Nobody argued. Nobody was ever going to argue.

So they asked Dov what it was like, because Dov had been there.

He had only been held a few days before Ysabel bought him, but a few days was enough. A rough place, he said. People treated badly. Being human made it easier than it was for the others — he did not seem comfortable saying that out loud, but he said it, because they had asked and it was true.

He had never counted more than **four or five guards** in the compound. Brutes rather than soldiers, in his estimation — nasty men who had found work that let them be nasty and be paid for it.

The one to worry about was the man in charge. **Vaskar.** Dov had never seen him fight. He had seen him standing still, which had been enough.

They talked about the tunnel. Erwana said Wolfram would fit, and then looked at Wolfram, and amended it: he would fit, but it would be tight.

Then Ysabel made her offer.

She would come with them. She would present herself at the gate as exactly what she is — a merchant of Solteres, arrived to buy stock for her mills, which she has done at that holdfast before and which nobody there would think twice about. They would go in as her staff. If JonLief's family were inside, she would simply buy them, and they would all walk out again with nobody's hand on a weapon. If his family were not inside, she was very well placed to ask questions.

In exchange, she wanted the ledger.

They made the bargain, with one condition of their own: **she gets the book when everybody is clear of Bramblefen. Not before.**

She agreed to that quickly, which told them something about how badly she wanted it.

---

### XI.

Then there was Erwana, still on the rope.

They cut her loose. Ysabel did not enjoy it and did not fight it.

They asked whether she would come with them and show them the ground, and the answer was in her face long before it was in her mouth. She had spent four weeks living on berries within a few miles of that place because she did not know where else in the world to be, and she was not going back through that wall for anything they could offer her.

So they gave her a pack with food in it — real food, as much as it would hold — and **five silver**, which was almost a third of all the silver the party had.

And they gave her directions.

**Go west, and then south, and ask for a village called Ald's Star.** Green halflings; you can't mistake it. The old man who runs it is called Ald. Tell him Wolfram sent you. They are Emperor-fearing and they will not turn you away, and they have room, because they lost two of their own not long ago.

She said the name back to make sure she had it. Then she said it again, walking, twice more before she was out of earshot.

Nobody watched her go for very long. There was a compound to get to.

---

### XII.

They made the fen by half past five in the afternoon.

Bramblefen sits low in a valley, which meant that for a good while before they reached it they could look down and see the whole of it laid out: a **fifteen-foot stone wall** in a rough ring, and four buildings inside it.

A large stone prison, old, built for that purpose by somebody who took the work seriously.

A smaller brick building beside it, converted to the same use less carefully.

A blacksmith's shop with an armory attached.

And a large house, well kept, where they were told the master of the place lives and keeps his office.

They counted the guards from the ridge, because Dov had told them four or five.

They counted **eight** without trying, and stopped being sure they had seen all of them.

There was also a work party of slaves in the middle of the yard, being directed to put something together. From that distance it was impossible to say what.

They looked at each other. Their information was a month old and it was wrong, and everybody understood that at the same moment.

They went anyway.

---

### XIII.

Ysabel walked up to the gate as though she owned the road and told the guards she was there to buy stock for her mills.

The guard on the left barely straightened up. Not today, he said. Come back tomorrow.

She asked why.

Because Lord Ironwake had sent his champion — **the Juggernaut** — down to Bramblefen, and there was a **sparring match at sundown** to warm him up for a trial by combat in the city in a few days' time. Everyone was going to watch. The guard was noticeably pleased about it, in the way of a man who has had a long month and is owed an evening.

They were putting him in against a gnome. One of the slaves. A stubborn little thing, apparently, that kept fighting back, and had been fighting back since the day it arrived, and was due to stop tonight.

Ysabel pressed. She is very good at pressing.

The gate opened.

---

### XIV.

Inside, the yard was working.

It was muddy underfoot and the air sat heavy and humid and full of the smell of a great many unwashed people who are frightened all of the time. It was loud — guards shouting across each other, hammering from the middle of the yard where the work party was driving in long sharpened stakes to make a ring, and under all of it a low sound that took a moment to identify as a man.

There was a slave tied to a post near the wall, and a guard beating him, and the guard was drunk and taking his time about it.

The man escorting them noticed them noticing.

"Don't trouble yourself. He's half orc. They take a beating." He said it comfortably, as a reassurance. "Won't kill him. Probably."

They walked on through it, because they had a part to play, and the whole of the yard was a thing happening to somebody else in front of them while they said nothing at all.

And in the middle of that noise, Wolfram happened to look twice at one of the men in the work party.

He did not know why.

He looked again, and the man was just a slave among slaves, thin and filthy like the rest of them, hauling a stake into place.

But something in the set of the shoulders, or the way the head turned, sat in Wolfram's memory like a name on the tip of a tongue — and would not come.

---

*The gate is shut behind them. The sun is going down. Somewhere in that compound there is a gnome who is not expected to see the morning, an old man who writes everything down, and a man Wolfram is certain he has seen before.*
