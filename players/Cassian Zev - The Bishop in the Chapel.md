# The Bishop in the Chapel
*For Channing. How Cassian Zev first met Bishop Osric — and the times since. Everything here is what Cassian saw and believes.*

---

### I.

The chapel at the training house was the only room in the building that was never locked, and the only room nobody visited at that hour.

Cassian visited it. He had visited it every night that month.

He had beaten Brother Hale in the yard that afternoon — Hale, who had nine years on him and a scar from a real fight, who taught the second-years and did not like to be taught — and he had beaten him twice, cleanly, the second time with the whole yard watching. The masters had said *good*, in the voice masters use when they mean *careful*. Hale had said nothing at all. And Cassian had gone in to supper with his hands still shaking, not from the fight but from the certainty, the plain physical certainty, that he had been made for something and had not yet been told what.

So he went to the chapel to ask.

It was cold. Two candles were still burning at the altar, which they were not supposed to be, and the sun of the Emperor hung over them in beaten brass, catching the light and giving it back softer. Cassian knelt at the front, on the stone, because the cushions were for people who were tired, and he prayed the way he had been taught: plainly, and without bargaining. *I am ready. Show me where.*

He did not hear anyone come in.

When he opened his eyes there was an old man sitting in the front pew, a little to his left, close enough to touch. Very old — older than anyone Cassian had ever seen walking — thin in the face, with a travelling coat over a churchman's black, dusty at the hem as though he had come a long way on foot. He sat the way a man sits in a room where he is expected, and he was looking at Cassian with an expression Cassian would spend a long time afterwards trying to name. The nearest he ever came was: *recognition*.

"Don't get up," the old man said. "You've been on your knees for an hour. Another minute won't hurt you."

Cassian got up anyway. It was what you did.

"Forgive me, Father. I didn't hear the door."

"No," the old man agreed, as though that settled something. "My name is Osric. I was a bishop once. I am still, in the only way that matters." He looked up at the brass sun over the altar, and something passed across his face that might have been fondness and might have been grief. "I have served the Emperor longer than you would believe if I told you the number, and nearer to him than any man now living. He has let me go on living, for a while longer than men are meant to. A reward for faith. Or a use for it. I have never been sure there is a difference."

Cassian had been raised on stories of men like that. Holy men whose devotion had been answered in the body. He had never once doubted that they existed. He had simply never expected to be sat beside one in a cold chapel at midnight, and to find that the man had dust on his coat.

"Why are you here, Your Grace?"

"Because you asked," said Osric. "Not many do. Most people pray to the Emperor the way they'd talk to the weather. You were talking to *him*." He tilted his head. "And because I watched you in the yard today."

"You were there?"

"I'm a great many places I'm not noticed." Osric smiled very slightly. "You beat a better-trained man twice, and the second time you did it in front of everyone because you wanted them to see. That is pride, and you'll want to watch it. But under the pride there is something else, and it's the thing I came for." He leaned forward a little, and for a moment the candlelight seemed to sit on him oddly, as though he were standing a step further away than the pew allowed. "You have it in you to be great, Cassian Zev. Not good. *Great*. The kind they put in the stories you were raised on. I am not here to tell you that you will be. I am here because I would like you to be, and I can help."

He held out his hand. In it was a string of prayer beads.

They were plain: dark wood, worn smooth as river stones, fifty or so on a cord that had been knotted and re-knotted by more than one pair of hands. At the end hung a small sun, the same sun as over the altar, in iron gone dark with handling. Cassian had seen finer beads on novices.

"These are old," said Osric, "and they've been prayed on by people who meant it, which is the only thing that makes beads worth anything. Pray with them when you need to know which way to go. Not when you want something — when you *need to know*. Sometimes you'll be answered. Sometimes you won't, and that will be an answer too." He pressed them into Cassian's palm and closed Cassian's fingers over them with his own, and his hand was dry and very light, like holding paper. "And when there is something that needs doing, I'll find you. I'll ask. You'll be free to say no."

"I won't say no."

"I know," said Osric, a little sadly. "That's rather the point of you."

Cassian looked down at the beads in his fist.

When he looked up, the pew was empty.

He did not hear the door that time either. He stood in the cold chapel for a long while, with the two candles burning lower, feeling the way you feel in the moment after you have been holding your breath without knowing it — and then he knelt again, on the stone, and said thank you, because that was also what you did.

---

### II.

The next morning in the yard, his blade did something it had never done.

It was the third pass of the drill, the one the masters used to tire you before they let you rest. Cassian brought his sword round on the line he had been taught, and as it came the steel *sang* — a thin high sound, felt more than heard — and a light ran down the edge of it like frost forming, gone before anyone else in the yard turned their head. The practice post split along the grain where the edge touched it.

He stood very still, and then he finished the drill, because it was the drill.

That night he counted the beads, one by one, and found there were fifty-three, and that the iron sun at the end was warm.

He told no one. Not because it was secret — a gift from the Emperor's own bishop was no shameful thing — but because he had a strong, plain sense that it was *his*, and that saying it aloud in the barracks would make it smaller. When the magic came more often after that, and the masters remarked on it, he told them it was a blessing from the Emperor. Which was true. He had it from the Emperor's own servant.

---

### III.

He has seen Osric twice since.

The first time was a feast day, in the refectory, the whole house at the long tables and the noise of two hundred people eating as if they had been starved. Osric was simply sitting across from him, between two second-years, where a moment before there had been a gap on the bench. He asked Cassian how the beads were. He asked after Brother Hale, by name, and said Hale was a better man than Cassian thought and would be useful to him one day. And while he spoke, the boy on his left reached across him for the bread — reached *through the space where his elbow was* — and did not so much as glance at him. Nobody did. Two hundred people, and not one of them looked at the very old man in the dusty coat sitting at the novices' table.

Cassian decided, then, that this was part of the gift. A bishop who walks with the Emperor goes where he likes and is seen by whom he chooses. It frightened him a little. He was ashamed of being frightened, and told no one that either.

The second time was the night before his posting.

The Office of the Lost Flock. The Shepherds. Cassian had read the order three times looking for the mistake in it. He had trained to stand in a line of holy warriors, and they were sending him to walk behind an old man in a violet cloak, collecting runaways. He was at the chapel again, not praying this time — only sitting — when the beads went warm in his hand, and Osric was in the pew beside him.

"You think it's a detour," said Osric.

"Isn't it?"

"Go where you're sent," said the old bishop. "Watch what you're shown. And be kind to the people they put in front of you — you'll find that harder than you expect, and it will matter more than you think." He was quiet for a moment. He looked, Cassian thought, more tired than before. Fainter, somehow, as if the candlelight were having to try. "Great things very rarely happen on the road you were expecting. Remember that, when it starts."

"When what starts?"

But the pew was empty.

---

### Now

Cassian carries the beads on his belt, where his hand can find them. He prays with them most nights, and on some nights the quiet that follows has a shape to it — a direction, a feeling of *this way* — and on others it is only quiet.

He has never told anyone about Bishop Osric. Not the masters. Not Baltus Thole.

He sees no reason he should. The bishop has asked nothing of him yet.

*Yet.*
