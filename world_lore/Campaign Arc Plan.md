### Established canon this plan must not contradict

Corrections from the DM, Aug 2026. These are settled at the table and cannot be retconned:

1. **The amulet of transportation has ONE charge left, not two.** It goes one way only — it returns everything in a 10-ft-diameter, 8-ft-high cylinder to the pentagram in **Room 3 of Saraswati's crypt on Dol Island**, from anywhere on any plane. The party knows all of this. See §6.
2. **Ald's Star is a village of roughly two dozen halflings** — plus, since Chapter 7, **Erwana and thirty freed slaves** sent there on Wolfram's name. Still too small for *Return of the Jailer* as written; §8.1a is how it keeps growing.

Corbin Wainwright's paintbrush is another.** The party knows none of this yet; Luthien has seen her once (Ch7). See §8.6.

## 1. The thing you've already built and may not have noticed

Every plot in this campaign is about **imprisonment and who holds the keys.**

- The party are property. 500 years, branded, with no way to check in and no way home.
- Saraswati is in a cell. Literally — chained in a tomb, then moved to a realm that *is* a prison.
- St. Pyotr's was a madhouse where the warden tortured inmates because he thought pain would evict what was inside them.
- Bramblefen held thirty people under two different lies about what a cage is.
- Ysabel's victory ends with her being married off as a *thing somebody owns*.
- The Stock/Terms split is a legal fiction about who counts as a person.
- Escaped Penitents become Husks — a mind is a cell too.
- The final boss is a **warden**. His realm is a prison. His title is the Jailer.
- Adephagia's whole method was never brute force. It was starving a rival until she could be *locked up*.

The finale is already written into the premise:

> **Four people who are legally property decide whether to unlock a prisoner, knowing the master they'd have to defy to do it is the church that owns them.**

Hold that sentence somewhere you'll see it. Every structural recommendation below is downstream of it.

## 2. Design principles

- **Consequences without announcement.** Hide decisions inside choices the party makes for other reasons, and collect on them later. *(Chapter 7 did this: Gimble freed → the match broke → Ysabel was never detained.)* Never explain the link out loud.
- **Design around a passive party, not against it.** They tend not to chase, open, or intervene. Write a branch for inaction, and **collect on passive choices** so they learn that not-choosing is a choice — the Patient is the big one.
- **Convert threads into assets** for the finale rather than forcing closure (§6).
- **The mana rules are mechanical.** Old gods run on worship and can be starved; that's why the finale can have a win condition that isn't a damage race (§8.2).
- **Prep in two layers** — DM Notes (why) and Run Sheet (at the table).
- **Underwood is a rift sign**, not a separate plot *(`Elizabeth and the Green.md` §4)*.

## 5. The bridge: how the slaver plot hands you the cosmic plot

This is the single most valuable thing in this document, so it gets its own section.

**Where did the barrels go?**

In Chapter 2 the cultists loaded barrels of Saraswati's blood onto a ship and sailed away. That blood is the fuel — *Rise of the Jailer* is explicit that the cult needed a sufficiently powerful energy source to give the Jailer physical form, and they now have a god's blood in casks. Those barrels are your finale's clock, and they have been unaccounted for since Chapter 2.

**Lord Ironwake controls all transport in and out of Solteres and taxes every shipment.**

Which means:

> **The barrels are in Ironwake's tax records.** Weight, date, destination, and the name the buyer gave.

Consequences of this one connection:

1. The slaver arc and the cosmic arc stop being two campaigns running in parallel. Taking down Ironwake *is* how they find the Hecatomb.
2. Chapter 7's ledgers, Chapter 8's wedding, and Chapter 10's raid on Ironwake all become cosmic-plot chapters without changing a word of their content.
3. The party's reason to go after a slaving lord and their reason to save the world are the same errand — which is exactly the kind of tight braid a finale needs.
4. It gives you a reason for Ironwake to be *afraid* of something, which makes him a better villain than pure greed does. He knows what shipped through his docks. He doesn't ask questions, and he's stopped sleeping well.

✅ **Done:** the Working Ledger records the women's direction — inland, east of Underwood *(`The Ashgate Purchases.md`)* — and the casks share a tariff-mark with them *(`The Barrel Trail.md` §8)*.

## 6. Thread ledger — close, convert, or leave open

The principle:

> **Convert open threads into assets in the final battle.** Every thread they close becomes something concrete they have in the Hecatomb. Every thread they leave becomes a hand that isn't there.

That way unresolved threads aren't loose ends — they're absences the players feel. And you never have to force closure on something the table didn't care about.

### 6.1 The amulet — one charge, one direction, ten feet across

I had this wrong in the first draft. The real version is better, and you should not soften it.

**What it does:** one use, ever. Everything inside a 10-ft-diameter, 8-ft-high cylinder is dumped at the pentagram in Room 3 of Saraswati's crypt, from anywhere on any plane. The party knows all three facts.

That is not a convenience. That is the last scene of your campaign:

- **It only gets them out, never in.** The way *in* is the rift — the Hecatomb, running, with the Jailer on the other side of it. So the shape of the finale is fixed and good: **you go in through the enemy's front door and you leave through hers.**
- **Ten feet is the whole drama.** A 10-ft circle holds four or five people standing close. Not the freed men. Not Ysabel's guards. Not everybody who walked in. Somebody counts heads while a god is coming, and somebody is left holding the floor so the circle can close. You do not have to write that scene — just build the room and let them do the arithmetic.
- **The destination is perfect and you should not change it.** It puts them in her cell. Whoever comes out with them arrives in the exact chamber where she was chained, bled, and left for 666 days. If Sara walks out of that pentagram, she is standing in her own tomb, free, for the first time in centuries. That is your closing image and it has been sitting in the Chapter 2 file the whole time.
- **The geography closes the loop.** Dol is three miles off the coast they've been walking since Chapter 3. They end the campaign marooned on the island where it started, watching the mainland, waiting for a boat. Let that epilogue be quiet.

**Two things to handle before it matters:**

**Tell them what it's for, early.** A party will absolutely burn a one-shot teleport to escape a bad fight in Chapter 11. That's not a gotcha you want to spring. Have someone they trust — the Emperor, or Corbin, which is much crueller — say it plainly and in advance: *that is the only door out of that place. Do not spend it on anything less.* Then it's a real choice they make with open eyes.

**Have a fallback ready in case they spend it anyway.** Pick one and hold it in reserve:

1. **Somebody holds the rift open.** It stays passable only while the Hecatomb runs, which means closing it is what banishes the Jailer — so the last one through has to close it from the wrong side. Grim, clean, and needs no new lore.
2. **Adephagia's price is passage.** She wants the Jailer contained more than she wants the party dead. She'll open a door. It will cost something none of them want to pay.
3. **The Emperor spends everything he has saved for a thousand years** on one door, and nobody finds out whether he survived it. Only use this once, and only if it buys the ending you want.

### Close before the finale

- **Rowan.** Barney Soot → Vaskar, Solteres (Ch8). Must resolve, and must be winnable and losable. Then guardianship *(`What To Do With Rowan.md`)*.
- **Ironwake.** He's the Act II boss. Kill, ruin, or flip him, but end him.
- **Ysabel.** ✅ Trial cancelled by her blackmail; Chapter 8 is the forced marriage — and the party may let it happen *(Ch8 plan §3)*.
- **Vozruk / the Church problem.** They cannot walk into the last act still technically deserters. Resolve it into either a blessing or a leash.
- **Underwood.** ✅ Merged into the cosmic plot as a **rift sign** *(`Elizabeth and the Green.md` §4)*; Virgil is there now. Close it as a rift sign.
- **The herald who escaped St. Pyotr's.** Bring him back and kill him properly. He's the party's only recurring named enemy on the cosmic side.
- **Corbin Wainwright.** See §8 — he's your mortal antagonist and he has to die or be redeemed in the finale, not offscreen.

### Deliberately leave open

- **The Emperor.** "May he rise again." He should be *more* mysterious after the finale, not less. See §7.
- **Adephagia.** She is designed to be un-killable — as long as people are people, she eats. She should walk away, satisfied, in a way that makes the players furious.
- **The Church itself.** The Grand Ecclesiarch, the Malformed Throne, the Demoncore. This is your sequel campaign, not this one's target.
- **The Jailer's return.** Both source modules are explicit: he can only be banished. Say so out loud in the epilogue.
- **The first Penitent team's dead (Gimble survived), and the *Hreowan*'s crew.** Best used as a glimpse — the party sees them, Faded, in the Prison Realm, and cannot help them. Don't resolve it. Let it be the price of the door.
- **The pickled cabbage.** Six jars, taken from a dead man's house. Never explain it. It's the best running gag you have.

## 7. The Emperor — a proposal

The old man at the Inn is currently a TODO, and he's your riskiest loose piece, because an unexplained all-powerful figure who thanks the party is one step from a deus ex machina.

Your own lore already solves him. Old gods run on worship-mana. The Cult of Azerai worshipped the First Emperor **as a god**. The Church of Azerai has, today, millions of worshippers across four dioceses and is the only legal faith in the world.

> **The Emperor is an old god, and the Church of Azerai is the largest mana engine in Doaden — and it is pointed at an abstraction instead of at him.**

That gives you:

- **Why he's unseen for a thousand years.** The Demoncore detonated in his capital. Whatever it did to him, he came out of it barely present — and the Church that had worshipped him rebuilt itself around a god with no face, with an Ecclesiarch on the throne. Every prayer in the Empire goes to a name that isn't quite his.
- **Why nobody can perceive him.** He can afford almost nothing. Being seen by four people cost him something.
- **Why "May he rise again" is everywhere.** It's the one scrap of real worship left, said by villagers who have no idea what they're maintaining. Every peasant who says it is feeding him a crumb.
- **Why he's surfacing now.** A Brudda Death coming through into Doaden would take the one thing he still has: people.
- **Why he can't just fix it.** He is the weakest interested party in the room. He can point, warn, and thank. That's the entire budget.

He should appear two or three more times before the finale, each time slightly more solid, and he should **never** solve a problem. His function is to explain the rules — the mana economy, what the Prison Realm actually is, what freeing Sara would cost — and to leave. If he does anything in the last chapter, make it one action that costs him everything he's saved for a thousand years, and make the party spend the rest of the campaign not knowing whether he survived it.

Leave him open. This is your next campaign.

### 8.1a Making Ald's Star big enough — without retconning anything

Two dozen halflings can't host a five-murder investigation. But you don't need to retcon the village, and you don't need to move the chapter. **You need Chapter 7 to grow it, on camera, as a consequence the party chooses.**

**The mechanism is already written into your Chapter 7 aftermath.** "Where do the freed go?" — option 4 is *go north/inland; needs supplies, a guide, and someone to lead them; Wolfram is the obvious candidate.* Wolfram knows exactly one settlement in this region that is remote, defensible, food-rich, and owes the party a debt.

**So: the freed go to Ald's Star** ✅ *(played — thirty of them, Ch7 Session 3).* And then it keeps growing, for reasons the campaign has already established:

- Word travels among slaves — Anselm's version of Wolfram's story "travels well." A place that takes in collared men becomes a destination.
- Underwood is under Church quarantine (unenforced) with a spreading spore plague. People leave.
- There's a war in the north, which is the stated reason the Church had no help to send.
- Ald's Star has the one thing refugees need and the region has plenty of: **food.** Their whole economy is a cabbage that will grow anywhere.

Eighteen months of that turns 24 people into **three or four hundred** — which is exactly the size you need, and is a believable curve. Do not push for a city; a frontier boomtown is better than New Heldon anyway.

**Compress the module's location list to match.** *Return of the Jailer* wants nine locations. A boomtown of 350 has: a tavern, the old village hall, a smithy, a market square, a granary, a new stockade and guardpost, and a half-built chapel. That's five murder scenes plus investigation stops, and it's all you need. Cut the stonemason guild.

**Swap the mine for the town's own diggings.** The Dragon's Eye Mine exists so the cultists can tunnel toward the catacombs and so Dolan can survive a "cave-in" and swear he heard chanting. A boomtown digs constantly — wells, drainage cuts into the fen side, foundations for the stockade. The diggers hit something old. There's a collapse. One man comes out and nobody believes him. Same scene, and it grows naturally out of the town's expansion rather than needing a mine to exist.

**What this buys you emotionally is the entire point.** The murders happen in a town **the party built** — Wolfram's people, the men they cut out of cages, living under Ald's roof. Their one unambiguously good act becomes the harvest. That is the grimdark thesis of this setting stated in one chapter, and you get it for free by letting Chapter 7's aftermath run.

**Casting the module's roles out of people they know:**

- **The five victims.** Draw from Bart, Matilda (the child who chattered at them all morning), William the horse-keeper, and faces from **the thirty freed at Bramblefen** *(`The Bramblefen Freed.md` — Kesh Vanner is the one named survivor available; invent the rest when you need them)*. Five names the table knows will wreck it. Use them.
- **Gorab, the framed red herring → Harl Crook.** The ex-guard who wore the collar he used to lock. Half the town already wants him dead, he has a guard's history, and the party freed him personally. When the evidence points at him, they will believe it. Then they have to decide what to do about it, in front of the men they freed, using the torture authority Vozruk gave them. That is the moral test of the finale and it costs you nothing to set up.
- **Ald** stays Ald: grieving, useful, and a second pressure valve if the party needs someone else to be wrong about.
- **Aquilon → Vozruk**, threatening to burn the town if it spirals. He means it. It's their town.

### 8.2 The win condition — and it is not damage

Do not run this as a damage race. A party of four to five at level 10–11 will not out-DPR a legendary Collector Entity, and the module tells you outright the Jailer can only be banished, never killed.

Instead — and this is the payoff of everything above:

> **The Hecatomb is made of Saraswati.** They harvested her blood in barrels in Chapter 2 to give the Jailer a physical form. The blood-pit anchoring him in Doaden is *her*. She's in the Prison Realm, and she is also, in a sense, in that pit.

Which means the final fight has an objective, and the objective is the campaign:

- **Free the prisoner and the warden loses his anchor.** Sara out of the pit = the Jailer loses lair actions, loses the Hecatomb, loses his grip on Doaden, and is banished. That's the win.
- **It's a job, not a slugfest.** Somebody holds the line, somebody works the pit. Every ally they earned in the last twelve chapters is a body that can do one of those two things.
- **And it makes the mana rules mechanical.** Sara comes back at whatever strength her mana pool allows. Every art-item recovered, every masterwork preserved rather than destroyed, every crowd Luthien ever played to — all of it is a dial the players turned without knowing. If they collected nothing, she comes out barely a ghost and they win ugly. If Luthien has been feeding that guitar for ten chapters, a four-armed goddess stands up out of the pit and the table loses its mind.

That's how you make a level-10 party plausibly survive a CR 21 concept. They don't beat him. **They pick the lock while somebody holds the door.**

### 8.3 Adephagia's offer — the third act's best scene

Adephagia is not the Jailer's ally. She made a *deal* to have Sara stashed in his realm. A Brudda Death loose in Doaden eats the thing she lives on: people, and their greed and lust and hunger. She would lose everything.

So, late in the campaign, she offers the party terms — triggered when she realises the Tormentors want permanent convergence, not the brief Aspect she paid for *(`The Three Faiths.md`)*. It forces the party to ally with the villain of Chapters 2 and 4, gives them intel they can't get any other way, and means the price of the finale is that Adephagia stays fed and stays free.

⚠️ **Not through the Patient.** The woman they left at St. Pyotr's belongs to Brudda Death, not Adephagia *(`The Patient.md`)*. **Who carries the offer is open — Decision 50** (recommended: Orla Miller or a Solteres pact-holder).

Let them hate that. The correct ending to this campaign is a win that costs.

### 8.4 Level and CR

- **Target level 10–11 at the finale.** That's Path of Penitence's own suggested cap, it's *Return of the Jailer*'s sweet spot, and it's about ten more chapters from where they are.
- **Use the CR 13 Jailer, not CR 21.** With lair actions, wave reinforcements, an objective clock, and a party split between fighting and working the pit, CR 13 will feel enormous. CR 21 is a TPK.
- **Keep the Blood Altar cover rule** (3/4 cover, 1d6 on a miss to shatter the altar) — it's a great tension mechanic and it doubles as a countdown on the party's own cover.
- Run the **Ruined Cathedral wave fight** and the **Descending Passage ambush** as written. They're solid, and the wave structure is what burns resources before the boss.

### 8.5 The setup checklist

Seven things must be true before you can open the final chapter. Prep against this list:

1. **They want Brudda Death personally.** They've heard the name at St. Pyotr's and read about him in the book. They need an injury with his name on it — the Patient, the lost Penitent team seen Faded in the Prison Realm, or a direct threat to Rowan.
2. **They know Sara is in there and want her out.** Wolfram already saw her face through a hole in a tomb — sad, terrified, alive. Bring that image back at least twice.
3. **They know where the Hecatomb is.** Via Ironwake's transport records → the barrels → St. Pyotr's. (§5)
4. **They still have the amulet, and they know what it's for.** One charge. In through the rift, out through the pentagram. Tell them in advance so they don't spend it in Chapter 11 (§6.1).
5. **They've banked assets.** The §6 conversion table. Track it on one page.
6. **The Church has taken a position.** Vozruk either sanctions this or hunts them for it. *(Decision 55. Baltus's "latitude" in Ch7 is the first sign.)*
7. **Level 10–11.**

> 🍄 **And one thing that is NOT on this list, deliberately: Virgil's vaccine.** *(`Elizabeth and the Green.md` §10.)* Under the containment rule the pandemic is Brudda Death's placed ordnance and it only fires when the rift opens — **so a vaccine defuses it before the finale happens.** That is a second way to win, it does not require the Hecatomb, and it means the party can lose the fight and still save the world. **Do not put it on the critical path. Let Virgil's player find it.**

### 8.6 The vessels, and the schedule for showing them

Three separate things have to reach the players, and the order matters: **the guitar first, Corbin second, the cosmology third.** Reveal the guitar and they *care*. Introduce Corbin and he becomes a rival for something they now own. Only then does the mana economy land, because by that point they have a stake in it.

#### Beat 1 — the guitar wakes. ✅ Played, Chapter 7.

Luthien played for the thirty freed at Bramblefen and saw a glowing woman with four arms, then six, at the edge of his sight. **He doesn't know who she was.** Wolfram saw the same woman through the tomb on Dol. **Let them compare notes on their own.**

#### Beat 2 — Corbin, in Solteres. Chapter 8.

**Run him from `Corbin Wainwright and Orla Miller.md` and Ch8 plan §10.** He finds Luthien by the sound of the guitar. Warm, genuinely useful, and the first person to treat Luthien as an artist. He notices the guitar and doesn't reach for it. **Why he wants them** is §9 of his file: *they have seen her and he never has*, and his own allies hid Dol from him. He explains what the book leaves out — mana as an economy — and can date Vasant Panchami.

**Which means Corbin teaches the party the cosmology he intends to use against them.** Make him likeable for three chapters and let them find out afterwards what he did to Fenna Pembrook.

#### The roster

**Source of truth: `The Art-Items.md`.** Six findable vessels, all her own bone: guitar (Luthien), brush (Corbin), mask (Orla, from Corbin), shears (Maelis Vire, Ysabel's dress house), chisel (Sera Oleander) and potter's rib (Hanne Vetter, the Patient) — the last two in the Herald's holding.

#### What the vessels do in the last room

This is the mana dial from §8.2, made concrete. **How much of Saraswati stands up out of that pit is a function of how many vessels the party gathered and how much good work got made and appreciated along the way.** Collect nothing and she comes out a ghost and the win is ugly. Collect most of them, with Luthien having played to real audiences for ten chapters, and a four-armed goddess gets up out of her own blood.

Which puts Corbin's brush in the finale as an object, not a symbol — and gives you the best available ending:

- **Kill Corbin, take the brush.** She comes out stronger. Clean, and a little hollow.
- **Turn Corbin.** He hands it over. He is her most devout living worshipper — he has murdered for her — and *belief given freely is worth more than an object taken*, by your own mana rules. She comes out strongest.

So the party can save the villain, and it isn't sentiment. It's the optimal play. Let them work that out for themselves with a god screaming in a pit.
