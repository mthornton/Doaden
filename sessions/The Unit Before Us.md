# The Unit Before Us

*The story of the five Penitents Vozruk sent to Dol Island before you, and of the one who came back.*

*A player handout. Nothing in here is a secret you have to keep from each other — but the man it happened to has not told you most of it, and may never tell you all of it.*

---

## I.

There were five of them, and they were branded in the same room you were.

The same forge. The same neat rack of irons with their ends all shaped the same way. The same guard with the same faded sun on his own shoulder saying the same thing he says to everyone, because he has said it so many times that it has stopped being a threat and become a piece of information: *on your feet, or as a corpse.*

They stood in front of the same oak desk afterward and were welcomed as lambs for the flock by a man who did not look up from his ledger while he said it. Five hundred years each. A pittance, really.

Finnian Quickstep outfitted them and shouted at them not to touch anything. Nobody touched anything. They were, on the whole, a more sensible group than yours.

The Church did not write their names down anywhere that survives. Units get numbers.

**Ottilie Marsk** had the number. She was forty-eight and she had been a real sergeant in a real army before whatever it was that put her in the camp, and she never told anyone which army or what it was. She was not warm. She was something better than warm: she was *reliable*, in a place where nothing else was, and four difficult people learned inside a week that if Ottilie said a thing would happen, it happened.

**Brother Aldous Penn** was sixty-one and had been a priest until the Church decided he wasn't. He still prayed. He prayed over meals nobody wanted, and over the sick, and over people who told him to stop, and he had a way of doing it that made even Ottilie let him finish. He was the closest thing the unit had to a healer and by a considerable margin the closest thing it had to a conscience.

**Sabin Ferrow** was a half-elf and a thief and had opinions about everything. He read locks, he read ground, he read rooms. He also could not stop talking, which everyone agreed was a fault right up until the nights got long and it stopped being one.

**Grigor Tell** was a half-orc, and enormous, and said perhaps forty words a week. He carried things. He carried Aldous more than once, over ground the old man's knees would not manage, and never once mentioned it afterward.

And there was a gnome.

He was the last one assigned and the smallest by a distance and nobody, in the beginning, thought about him much at all. He was quiet in a way that read as timid until you had known him a month, at which point you understood it was something else entirely and you had simply been wrong.

His name was **Gimble Beren**.

---

## II.

They went out of the Castur in early summer, on a chartered boat with a hired crew, and the sea was kind to them the whole way, which they took at the time for luck.

Dol came up out of the water on the twentieth day: a green rock with a white collar of surf and a reef around it that the master swore at for an hour before he found a way in.

They went ashore in the ship's boat, in daylight, with everything they owned.

The boat pulled back out to the ship. The ship stood off. They watched it for a while from the sand, the way you do, and then they turned around and got on with the job.

Sabin looked back once more, an hour later, from higher ground, and the ship was gone.

He mentioned it. Ottilie said it would be standing off the far side out of the swell and would come back around at evening. That was a reasonable thing to say and she believed it when she said it.

It did not come back around at evening, or the next evening, or ever. Nobody has established whether that was arranged in advance by people who did not want a Church unit leaving that island, or whether it was five sailors deciding that whatever was on Dol was not worth what they were being paid. Both are possible. Neither helped.

By the third day they had stopped discussing it, because there was nothing to discuss, and because the island had begun handing them other things to think about.

---

## III.

There were footprints in the sand and a stone path under it, and the path went up through the scrub to a cliff face that somebody had spent a lifetime carving.

Sabin found the stair. He also found the mosaics, and he made them all stop and look, which cost an hour they did not have and which none of them ever regretted. Glass, thousands of pieces of it, dirty and chipped and still catching the light: landscapes, patterns, and one long sequence running down the wall panel by panel that told a story.

A village with black crops. A woman weeping over a child. Knights coming back with their shields broken. A girl who was not a knight, going up a mountain with a book of herbs in her hand.

"She wins," said Sabin, four panels down.

"How can you tell?" said Aldous.

"Because somebody spent a year on this wall. Nobody spends a year on a wall for a story where the girl loses."

The stair came out into a cave with a hole in the roof, and a column of sunlight coming down through it, and an island in the middle of the cave standing over a drop that went past where the light reached.

And bones. Two dozen people, near enough, scattered rather than laid out — which is the difference between a massacre and a burial.

Grigor crouched over them a long time. It was the most attention any of them had ever seen him give anything.

"No weapons," Ottilie said, eventually. "Not one."

"Not soldiers, then."

"No."

Aldous prayed over them. It took him a while, because he insisted on doing it properly, and by the end of it Sabin had stopped making faces and was standing with his hands folded like everyone else.

There was an apple tree growing on that island, improbably healthy, heavy with fruit, entirely unbothered by whatever had happened around its roots.

They filled their packs. Five hundred years of service comes with no meal plan.

---

## IV.

Here is the part that matters, and the part that is hardest to explain to anyone who wasn't there:

**they won.**

They went down into that crypt and they were good at it. Sabin got them past the door. Ottilie put them through the rooms in an order that made sense. Grigor stood in the doorways. Aldous kept them upright. Gimble went first into the dark places, over and over, because he could see in it and because he was small enough to fit where the trap was not aimed.

They fought things wrapped in linen that came off the walls, and they put them down. They found men in dark robes going about their business in the lower chambers — six, or seven, nobody agreed afterward — and the men in dark robes were not expecting anyone, and it was over quickly and it was not close.

And then it was quiet, and they stood in a stone room under an island with their own blood on them and other people's, and Ottilie did the arithmetic out loud.

Find out who is operating here. Eliminate them.

Both clauses. Done.

They were, by any reading of their orders, finished. They had completed the mission. On the strength of it, when they got home, five hundred years was going to come down by something — nobody knew how much, because nobody had ever met a Penitent who'd had it come down — but *something*.

Sabin said they should get to the beach and start building a signal fire and be ready to flag anything that passed.

Ottilie said no, and her reason was correct, and it killed all of them.

"We don't know that we got all of them," she said. "We know we got the ones who were here. If a ship comes in next week with twenty more, and we're sitting on the sand waving at it, then we haven't eliminated anybody. We've just cleared out a room for them."

So they stayed.

---

## V.

It was Aldous who found the tomb, on the second week, in a chamber they had already been through twice.

It was ornate — worked all over with the same patient skill as the mosaics on the stair — and somebody had chipped a hole in the side of it.

There was blood coming out of the hole. Slowly. Steadily. It was not old.

He put his eye to the gap.

There was a woman in there.

She was not bone and she was not dust. She lay in a gown that had been white and was not any more, and there was no decay on her at all, and her wrists were bound in chains gone black with age. All four of them.

She had four arms.

There was a wound open in her side, and it had plainly been open a very long time.

And then she looked at him.

Her eyes came round and found his through a gap the width of two fingers, as though she had known exactly where he would be standing, and there was nothing in them like anger. There was sorrow, and there was a fear so old that it had gone quiet, the way fear does when it has had years to settle in and make itself at home.

Her lips moved. No sound came with it.

Aldous Penn had spent forty years telling people about a god he had never once seen. He was sixty-one years old and he had just been looked at by something in a box.

When the others came, there was nothing in the tomb at all.

---

## VI.

He did not stop talking about it.

The Church teaches that the old gods never existed. Aldous had said so from a pulpit, more than once, in the years when he was allowed a pulpit. And now he had seen one chained up in the dark with a hole cut in her side and somebody bleeding her like a barrel, and every part of him that had ever been a priest reorganised itself around one idea.

She had to come out.

Ottilie said no. She said no on the first night and the second and the fourth. Her reasons did not change and they were not stupid:

They were four people and a gnome on a rock with no boat. Whatever was in that box had been put there by something strong enough to put it there, and had been kept there for a long time by people who had gone to considerable trouble. Opening it was not in anyone's orders. And the last thing five convicts needed, with five hundred years each hanging over them, was to be the ones who let something out.

"She is a prisoner," Aldous said. "So are we. That is the entire argument and you know it."

"That is a very good line, Brother. You've had two days to work on it."

"I've had sixty-one years."

Sabin sided with Ottilie because Sabin wanted to go home. Grigor said nothing and everyone assumed that meant Ottilie, and nobody ever asked him, and now nobody can.

Nobody asked Gimble either.

They were still arguing about it on the morning the ships came.

---

## VII.

Three of them. Not one — three, standing in past the reef in a line, with boats already going down the sides before the anchors were in.

Better than twenty men. And at the front of them, stepping onto the sand as though he had never hurried anywhere in his life, a very tall, very pale man in an ornate headdress.

The unit was underground. By the time they came up they were between the cliff and the beach with people on both sides of them.

Grigor put three men down and took a bolt through the thigh doing it.

And then Ottilie Marsk looked at the ground and the numbers and her own people, and she put her sword in the sand.

You can think what you like about that. Ottilie had been a real sergeant in a real army, and a real sergeant knows the difference between a fight and an execution, and does not spend the people she is responsible for on the second one out of pride.

She was expecting to be killed. All of them were.

**That is not what happened, and that is the part Gimble has never been able to put down.**

They were not killed. They were chained.

Because the men on that beach were not there to kill intruders. They were there to fill barrels. And somebody had just walked five healthy adults into their arms — people with strong hearts and clean blood, and, better than that, people nobody would come looking for.

Because who reports a missing Penitent?

A Penitent is a debt with legs. When a unit stops writing, the Church does not send anyone. The Church files it.

---

## VIII.

It took four days.

They were kept above the tide line on the far beach, chained to stakes driven into the sand, in the sun, within sight of the barrels and within sight of each other. It was done a little at a time. The men doing it were not in a hurry and were not angry and did not enjoy it particularly. It was work. They talked about other things while they did it.

Once, one of them shouted a name at the sky in a voice like a prayer — *Adephagia* — and the others laughed, and it was clear it was a joke of some kind, and it was clear it was not entirely a joke.

**Aldous went first.** He was the oldest and they judged, correctly, that he would last the least. He tried to preach at them. He got a long way into it. He was still going, in a thin voice with long gaps in it, when he stopped.

**Sabin talked for three days.** At the guards, at the sky, at Grigor, at anyone. About his mother, about a job in Solteres that had nearly worked, about the mosaics on the stair and how she wins, she definitely wins, you could tell from the panel with the griffin. He got quieter each day, which was the only way any of them could measure how much was left of him.

**Grigor lasted longest.** There was more of him. He never said a word about it.

**Ottilie went last, and she went without giving them one thing.** Not a sound, not a plea, not a name. At the end she turned her head and looked directly at a particular spot in the treeline above the beach — the spot where she knew a gnome was lying in the ferns, watching, because she had counted her people every day of her life and she knew exactly where the fifth one was.

She looked at it for about two seconds.

Then she looked away, deliberately, and did not look at it again, because she was not going to be the one who told them where he was.

---

## IX.

They put Gimble last, and they put him aside, and both of those things were insults.

He was small. Barely worth the walk to the barrel, one of them said, in front of him, the way you'd say it about a chicken.

And they could not read him. He would not scream and he would not beg and he would not stop watching them, and men doing that kind of work do not enjoy being looked at steadily by something they cannot get a measure of. So they moved his stake further down the sand, away from the others, and got on with the ones that mattered.

Four days.

Four days was three too many.

How he got out of the cuff is his own business and he has never said. There is a version with a dislocated thumb in it and a version with a pin in it and a version where the tide did most of the work, and he has told none of them.

**What he did next was not escape.**

He got into the treeline and he stayed there. Two more days. Close enough to watch, and to hear, because Ottilie was still alive, and because he had not yet accepted that there was nothing he could do about that.

He was right that there was nothing he could do.

He has not accepted it.

---

## X.

When it was finished and the ships were gone he came down onto the sand and he did what he could, which was almost nothing, and took longer over it than was safe.

Then he went along the shore.

The reef around Dol has been eating ships for a thousand years and the beaches on the windward side are full of what's left of them — grey timber, half-buried, bleached to bone. He spent a day dragging pieces of other people's disasters into a heap and lashing them together with what he could find, and the result was a raft in the sense that it floated and in no other sense at all.

He put out into three miles of open water on it, alone, at first light, because staying was worse.

He made the mainland.

He walked for days after that with no map, no coin, and no idea which direction a Church camp lay in. He ate what a ranger eats when there's nothing, which is not much and not often. He slept badly. He asked directions from people who had never heard of the Castur River.

And then he found people who offered to help.

They gave him food. They were pleasant about it. He was so tired by then that pleasant was enough.

He worked out what they were before they got the irons on him, which is why the first three of them went down. He fought them there, and then again on the road when the wagon stopped, and again at the gate at Bramblefen, and again inside it. He was not going to win any of those and he knew it and he did them anyway, and that is the entire reason for everything that happened to him afterward.

---

## XI.

Vaskar the Brand looked at his new property and found a problem.

The gnome was too willful to break — Vaskar had a great deal of experience in breaking people and could tell inside a week when it was not going to take. He was too capable to put in a household; he'd kill somebody's cousin inside a month. And he could not simply be disposed of, because Vaskar prices everything and wasting an asset offends him.

And there was the shoulder.

There was a mark burned into it. A stylized sun, with a single tear falling from its centre.

**Vaskar knew exactly what that was**, and the knowledge sat in his stomach like cold water. Slavery is illegal by ruling of the Church of Azerai. Everybody ignores that, and everybody can afford to ignore it, right up until the day the Church finds one of its own servants in a collar. On that day Lord Ironwake's name and Lord Ironwake's money and Lord Ironwake's friends in Solteres would be worth precisely nothing, and Vaskar would be the man standing closest.

So: could not be sold. Could not be freed. Could not be killed without waste, and could not be kept.

Then a wagon came up the fen road with six of Lord Ironwake's own men riding alongside it and something the size of a cart horse chained in the back, and the problem solved itself.

Ironwake's champion needed practice. Not a real fight — a *sharpening*. Something quick and skilled and clever enough to make the big man work, and small enough to be no danger at all.

They riveted an iron mask over the whole of the gnome's head, so that no buyer coming through the yard would ever see a face, and left him in rags, so that nobody would ever look twice at a shoulder.

They stopped using his name. They stopped asking it.

The match is at sundown. Every guard who walks past the Deep Cages says the same thing, and they say it in front of him, because they do not believe there is anything in there that minds:

*That one'll be dead by dark.*

---

## XII.

He is not dead by dark.

He is sitting cross-legged in the exact centre of a stone floor, in the second cell of the Deep Cages, facing the door.

He was facing the door before it opened.

---

*Four names have never been written down anywhere that matters: Ottilie Marsk. Aldous Penn. Sabin Ferrow. Grigor Tell.*

*Somebody should probably write them down.*
