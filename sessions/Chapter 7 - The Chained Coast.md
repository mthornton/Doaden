Chapter Seven: The Chained Coast


Part One — The Road to Bramblefen

By the last week of the road they had stopped being two groups sharing a fire.

It had happened without anyone deciding it. Symon Reeve knew which of the horses would bite and had opinions about all of them. Cael Ordway, who was eighteen and desperate not to look eighteen, had taken to setting out the party's bowls before his own. Mina Dorn said perhaps forty words a day and thirty of them were useful. Perrin Aske, who had run Lady Ysabel's father's household before he ran hers, stood a little behind her shoulder at all times, in a way that had clearly been trained into him so long ago that he no longer knew he was doing it.

And Dov Brandt had begun following Wolfram around.

Not obviously. He asked about footwork. He asked how you set a shield when a thing bigger than you is coming, and he listened to the answer as though it were going to be on a test. He had carried a spear in a town militia once, some years ago, and he mentioned it twice, and both times he was careful to say it had not been much of a militia.

Wolfram, who had not been looked at that way by anyone since Westfield, answered every question seriously.

That was where they were on the last night: a fire, a cart, eleven people, and Solteres two days off.

---

Ysabel had grown quieter each day as the smoke on the horizon got closer, and on the last night she gave up being quiet.

"I beg you to reconsider."

She was not performing. That was the difficulty with her; she never was.

"When I arrive at that trial without a champion, Lord Ironwake will put his own forward, and I will have to stand against him. His champion is not a normal man. He is easily twice the size of the largest man I have ever seen."

Wolfram looked up from the fire.

"Twice the size of *me*?"

"No." Ysabel considered him for a moment, quite honestly, with her head tilted. "You are not the largest man I have ever seen. He is perhaps three times your size."

Nobody said anything for a while after that.

They talked it round anyway, because it deserved talking round. Luthien pointed out — reasonably, and without much heat — that three of the four of them had no business in an arena, and that the fourth would be walking into it against something three times his size. JonLief asked whether magic was permitted in a trial by combat. It was, Ysabel said. It changed nothing.

They asked her what the thing actually *was*, and she told them, and it was worse than they expected.

Trials by combat in Solteres are held in the coliseum, on festival days. The whole day is given over to entertainment — plays, acrobats, animal acts, a dozen other things — and the trial is the last event on the card. It is guaranteed to be brutal and it is guaranteed to end in a death, which is precisely why it is scheduled last. The crowds like it.

"So you would be the finale," said Luthien.

"Yes."

"After the acrobats."

"Yes."

---

She told them the rest of it that night as well, and she told it plainly, which did not help her.

She had begun as an importer of fine cloth — exotic fabrics out of far-off places, sold on to the dressmakers of Solteres. Some of those places it is not legal to import from. So she had the goods smuggled, and that is how she first met Lord Leofard Ironwake, who controls what comes into that city and what leaves it, and who takes his cut of everything, and who takes rather more of the illegal things.

She paid his taxes for a while. Then she decided she was clever enough not to.

He found out inside a month. He seized an entire shipment and sent word that a second discourtesy would end with her dead in a way people would talk about.

So she changed the shape of the business. She built a mill and wove her own cloth from raw material she brought in herself, worked by slaves she bought at a holdfast in the fens called Bramblefen. Her margins doubled. Then she opened a second house making finished dresses, and the dresses were better than anything else in the city, and within a season every noblewoman and every merchant's wife in Solteres wanted one and would pay any price asked. She asked high prices. She went from wealthy to one of the wealthiest women in the city.

And then, being that wealthy, she decided she was finally too big for Ironwake, and she smuggled a shipment past him a second time.

He had her niece poisoned, and arranged the evidence so that Ysabel would hang for it.

She finished the account and looked at them and waited.

---

It was hardest for Wolfram, and everyone at that fire knew it, including Ysabel.

He is a protector of the weak. It is not a thing he decided to be; it is the shape he comes in, and refusing a frightened woman who has asked four times goes against the whole of him.

But he is also a man under orders, and the Church did not send him across a continent to die in a coliseum for a merchant he has known for three weeks.

Luthien's decision cost him nothing at all. He said so.

JonLief and Virgil were practical about it in the same breath and for the same two reasons: they had orders, and none of them would survive the fight.

So the answer stayed no, for the fifth time, and the fire burned down, and it was agreed that they would see her as far as the gates of Solteres and no further. After that they would find the Church's house in the city and report in, and stop being men nobody had heard from in two and a half months.

That was the plan when they went to their bedrolls.

It lasted until dawn.

---

They woke to shouting.

One of Ysabel's men — the one who had drawn the last watch — came to the ashes of the fire dragging a rope, and on the end of it was a woman in rags with both fists still full of last night's supper.

"Caught this one with her hands in the pot. Been at the leavings like a dog."

She went down on her knees before anyone asked her to.

"Please. Please don't hurt me. It's only what you left, I swear it — I waited till you were all abed, I never came near you. I only wanted what was going in the dirt anyway." She was shaking. "I swear it to the Emperor. It's been days with nothing but berries."

In the grey light they could see her properly. Middle-aged. Filthy past the point where filth is a condition and into where it is a covering. And under it, unmistakably, the soft mild markings of Tabaxi about the nose and the ears — half, and not enough of it to be one thing or the other.

Ysabel looked at her for about a second and a half.

"Don't trouble yourselves. It's a slave, and a runaway, and nothing it says will be worth the hearing." She turned to her steward without raising her voice. "Perrin, keep it tied. We'll take it into Solteres with us and hand it back to one of Lord Ironwake's men. It won't undo what's between us, but he may remember the courtesy."

*It.* Twice, in the space of a breath, without any particular malice, the way you would talk about a lost dog with a collar on.

JonLief crouched down instead, so that his eyes were level with hers, and asked her where she had come from.

Her name was Erwana.

She had come from Bramblefen.

---

The story came out slowly and in the wrong order, because she was starving and roped and had learned across thirty years that volunteering anything is how you get hurt.

There is an old man at Bramblefen. Old Cobb. He is the only permanent slave in the place — the bookkeeper, who has recorded every purchase and every sale there for longer than anyone can remember. He told her about a drainage grate at the back of the pens, and the smuggler's channel underneath it that runs beneath the wall and out into the fen.

And he did not send her out empty-handed.

For years Cobb had been quietly keeping a second set of books — a true copy, made in secret, of the sales that the men who own that place would most prefer forgotten. He gave it to her the night she ran and asked her to get it out of the fen, and told her to go as far from Solteres as her legs would take her and then find a Church, any Church, and put the book in their hands.

Not the Church in Solteres. That one, he said, looks the other way about slavery and has done for years. But some other city, some other bishop. He believed the Church was good underneath. He believed somebody in it would do something.

She did not know what. She has never read a word of it. She cannot read.

She offered it to them there, on her knees on the wet ground: food, some coin, and let her run, and the book was theirs.

They agreed.

---

JonLief opened it himself.

It ran back years. Names, prices, buyers, dates, in a small precise clerk's hand that got shakier as the pages went on. The last entry was about six weeks old, and then the writing simply stopped, mid-page, as though the man holding the pen had heard something in the corridor.

Two months back, he found this:

*An elf woman and her son, brought in to Bramblefen.*

*Elizabeth. Rowan.*

The ages were right.

He asked Erwana whether she knew anything, and she said no — and then stopped, and looked at the pendant hanging at his throat, and went very still.

"I knew that."

She said it twice.

"I knew that. She had one. The same. She was in a cell near mine and she was so hot with the fever you could feel it across the straw, and she coughed, all night, every night, and there was a boy with her and she would not let go of him." She looked up. "The guards came one day and took them both out. I don't know where. That's all. That's all I know, I swear it."

---

There was more, and she gave it without being pressed, once she understood they wanted it.

All the women in that holdfast were sold. All of them, in one lot, not long after the guards took the elf woman and the boy away.

All except her.

The guards had laughed about that where she could hear. She smelled so bad, they said, that even filth like *those* wouldn't take her.

The buyers were a small group of men, and she had never seen anything like them. They kept their hoods up. What she could see of them — hands, wrists, the sides of their faces — was covered in scars, old ones and new ones layered over each other, more than any accident or any beating accounts for.

They paid, and they took the women, and Erwana never heard another word about any of them.

She does not know whether the elf woman was part of that sale.

Nobody around that fire said anything for a moment. Three of them had stood on a beach on Dol Island and watched a boat pull away with barrels in it, and none of them said that out loud either.

---

Through all of it, Ysabel kept putting her hand out.

She wanted the book. She saw what it was inside about four seconds, which is roughly four seconds faster than anyone else at that fire: the names of politicians, noblemen and merchants who had bought people. Not that anyone in Solteres greatly minds a man buying people. But a *second* set of books means somebody was hiding something, and men do not go to the trouble of hiding a thing unless they would be damaged by its coming out. With that ledger she could squeeze every name in it, and every name in it could squeeze Ironwake, and the charges against her might quietly evaporate.

And in between reaching for it she kept talking about Erwana as though Erwana were furniture.

A slave is ignorant. A slave is not to be trusted. Subhumans are below humans and are suited to nothing better than serving those above them — and slavery does a genuine good for humans too, when applied to criminals, since it gives a man a way to pay what he owes.

"Dov is mine for three more years," she said, by way of illustration.

And she gave them his history, briefly, the way you would describe a horse's bloodline. Gambling debts. A robbery attempted on the Solteres road and very badly attempted. His family's property sold to make restitution, and then five years — which he had discharged, lawfully and by his own choice, by offering himself for sale. She had bought him. He had three years left. He would walk away clear at the end of it, she pointed out, which is more than a cell would have given him.

Nobody made a scene about it.

That is worth writing down accurately: nobody made a scene about it. Wolfram did not say anything to her. Nobody stood up. It was early and cold and there was a great deal else happening.

But that was the moment the arithmetic finished itself, quietly, in four heads at once.

Dov Brandt was owned, for a fixed term, on paper, for a crime, by a woman being perfectly reasonable about it over breakfast.

And each of them owed the Church of Azerai five hundred years.

Dov had three left.

---

The plan changed inside a minute of JonLief reading that entry. He was going to Bramblefen. If Elizabeth and Rowan were still there he was taking them out, and if they were not he was finding out where they had gone.

Nobody argued. Nobody was ever going to argue.

So they asked Dov what it was like, because Dov had been there.

He had only been held a few days before Ysabel bought him, but a few days was enough. A rough place, he said. People treated badly. Being human made it easier than it was for the others — he did not seem comfortable saying that out loud, but he said it, because they had asked and it was true.

He had never counted more than four or five guards in the compound. Brutes rather than soldiers, in his estimation — nasty men who had found work that let them be nasty and be paid for it.

The one to worry about was the man in charge. Vaskar. Dov had never seen him fight. He had seen him standing still, which had been enough.

They talked about the tunnel. Erwana said Wolfram would fit, and then looked at Wolfram, and amended it: he would fit, but it would be tight.

Then Ysabel made her offer.

She would come with them. She would present herself at the gate as exactly what she is — a merchant of Solteres, arrived to buy stock for her mills, which she has done at that holdfast before and which nobody there would think twice about. They would go in as her staff. If JonLief's family were inside, she would simply buy them, and they would all walk out again with nobody's hand on a weapon. If his family were not inside, she was very well placed to ask questions.

In exchange, she wanted the ledger.

They made the bargain, with one condition of their own: she gets the book when everybody is clear of Bramblefen. Not before.

She agreed to that quickly, which told them something about how badly she wanted it.

---

Then there was Erwana, still on the rope.

They cut her loose. Ysabel did not enjoy it and did not fight it.

They asked whether she would come with them and show them the ground, and the answer was in her face long before it was in her mouth. She had spent four weeks living on berries within a few miles of that place because she did not know where else in the world to be, and she was not going back through that wall for anything they could offer her.

So they gave her a pack with food in it — real food, as much as it would hold — and five silver, which was almost a third of all the silver the party had.

And they gave her directions.

Go west, and then south, and ask for a village called Ald's Star. Green halflings; you can't mistake it. The old man who runs it is called Ald. Tell him Wolfram sent you. They are Emperor-fearing and they will not turn you away, and they have room, because they lost two of their own not long ago.

She said the name back to make sure she had it. Then she said it again, walking, twice more before she was out of earshot.

Nobody watched her go for very long. There was a compound to get to.

---

They made the fen by half past five in the afternoon.

Bramblefen sits low in a valley, which meant that for a good while before they reached it they could look down and see the whole of it laid out: a fifteen-foot stone wall in a rough ring, and four buildings inside it.

A large stone prison, old, built for that purpose by somebody who took the work seriously.

A smaller brick building beside it, converted to the same use less carefully.

A blacksmith's shop with an armory attached.

And a large house, well kept, where they were told the master of the place lives and keeps his office.

They counted the guards from the ridge, because Dov had told them four or five.

They counted eight without trying, and stopped being sure they had seen all of them.

There was also a work party of slaves in the middle of the yard, being directed to put something together. From that distance it was impossible to say what.

They looked at each other. Their information was a month old and it was wrong, and everybody understood that at the same moment.

They went anyway.

---

Ysabel walked up to the gate as though she owned the road and told the guards she was there to buy stock for her mills.

The guard on the left barely straightened up. Not today, he said. Come back tomorrow.

She asked why.

Because Lord Ironwake had sent his champion — the Juggernaut — down to Bramblefen, and there was a sparring match at sundown to warm him up for a trial by combat in the city in a few days' time. Everyone was going to watch. The guard was noticeably pleased about it, in the way of a man who has had a long month and is owed an evening.

They were putting him in against a gnome. One of the slaves. A stubborn little thing, apparently, that kept fighting back, and had been fighting back since the day it arrived, and was due to stop tonight.

Ysabel pressed. She is very good at pressing.

The gate opened.


Part Two — The Yard

The inner yard of Bramblefen was mostly mud.

It had been a prison once, built for that and nothing else, and then converted to the housing of people waiting to be auctioned, which required no conversion at all. What grass there was grew in patches, and the patches were trodden into the same brown as everything else.

Directly ahead of them stood the big one: three storeys of discoloured stone with moss in the cracks, old, and solid, and squatting in the middle of that valley like something that had been dropped there. The Deeps, the guards called it.

And it gave off something.

None of them said so at the gate. They did not need to. Standing in front of that building, you could feel the despair coming out of it the way you feel heat off a wall at evening — the accumulated misery of thousands of people shut inside it across centuries, pressed into the stone and still there.

Wolfram had felt it before. They all had. It was St. Pyotr's, and it was weaker than St. Pyotr's, and it was unmistakably the same thing.

The other three buildings ringed the yard. A two-storey house to the right of the Deeps, which stood out immediately for being the only structure in the compound in good repair. A small blacksmith and armory, with no smoke at the forge chimney and the front door chained and padlocked. And a second barracks for the holding of slaves — smaller than the Deeps, and less deliberate about it, and no better for that.

Eight guards, they had counted from the ridge. Those were only the ones standing in the open.

---

The yard was working.

A group of slaves was being driven to assemble a fence out of long sharpened timbers, in a rough circle in the centre of the mud, and the work was about half done. The guards running it were brutal in a way that had nothing to do with efficiency. Every few minutes the work stopped because somebody had been knocked down for an imagined disobedience, and from the laughter it was plain that the beatings were the point — each man trying to go one better than the last.

A half-orc was tied to a post by the wall with a drunken guard clubbing him between pulls at a flask.

"Don't trouble yourself about that," said the guard escorting them, comfortably, as a reassurance. "Orcs can take a beating. Not likely to do any permanent damage."

They walked on through it, because they had a part to play, and the whole of that yard was a thing happening to other people in front of them while they said nothing at all.

---

The work stopped when the slaves noticed them, and started again inside a second, because the guards saw to it.

One man did not start again.

He dropped the timber he was carrying. It landed in the mud and he just stood there, looking, with an expression on his face that had nothing to do with fear — shock, and something underneath the shock that was much worse to see on a slave, because it was hope.

A guard was on him immediately, beating him to the ground and kicking him as he curled up.

In the one moment the man's face had been visible, Wolfram knew him. He could not put a name to it. But it was somebody from Westfield.

Wolfram moved before he had finished deciding to.

"I want to inspect this man," he said, kneeling down beside him. "He might be somebody we want to purchase."

The guards, who had no instruction covering this, stepped back.

The man opened his eyes. He was perhaps sixty, with the frame of someone who has done hard labour for a long time and recently done it on no food.

"Wolfram. What are you doing here?"

"I'm sorry. I recognise you, but I don't remember who you are."

"We don't have much time. My name is Anselm. I worked for your father. After your arrest—"

The boot came in and took the rest of it.

JonLief saw what was needed and started a conversation three yards away, loudly, asking the nearest slaves about an elf woman and a child — which did exactly what he intended. Guards converged on him to explain that these slaves were busy and that visitors saw Vaskar first.

Which gave Wolfram one more moment, bent over a man in the mud.

"After your arrest I went to the Bishop and told him what I heard. About your reasons. The Bishop never even wrote down what I said." A breath. "I'm sorry. But we need your help. We're going to break out of here tonight. Everybody will be watching the fight. Once it starts, we go for the guards and we earn our freedom or we die trying. There are weapons in the armory. If you could get some of them for us, it would make a great deal of difference."

A guard stepped over and kicked Anselm in the side of the head, which ended it.

"Sir. You need to see Vaskar before you inspect any of the slaves."

Wolfram and JonLief walked back to the others and passed it along quietly, a few words at a time, over the course of crossing a yard.

---

A guard met them at the front of Vaskar's house and told them they were expected — that the master had already been informed of their arrival and would see them now. He looked at the size of the group and said that Lady Ysabel's people would have to wait outside.

Ysabel countered without pausing, and brought in her assistant, JonLief, and Virgil.

"Wolfram, stay with the group." She was already moving. "Be sure to keep my handmaiden Mina safe."

Inside, in the hall, there was a man in a chair with a tarpaulin thrown over him.

That was all. A chair, a shape under canvas, and spatters of blood up the wall behind it. Under the tarp the shape was breathing, slowly and with difficulty, and the sound filled the room.

Vaskar did not acknowledge it. He did not look at it, or apologise for it, or explain. He simply beckoned them to follow him up to the balcony on the second floor, and they followed him, past it.

---

"Welcome to Bramblefen, Lady Ysabel."

He boomed it, from the balcony rail, in the tone of a man enjoying a joke nobody else had been told.

"I am so happy to see you. Are you here for more slaves for your factories? Or perhaps to purchase a new champion? I hear you may be in need of one."

"You needn't trouble yourself about my champion." She did not rise to it, exactly; she met it. "Yes. Ulfric is dead. I have others. And the Emperor knows I am innocent — his light will shine on whatever champion I select, and that champion will prevail in the trial."

Vaskar laughed at her. Genuinely, with pleasure, three syllables of it.

"Nobody has a chance against the Juggernaut."

"I am not here to discuss my personal affairs." She moved on as though the laughing had not happened. "I need stock for my mills. Seamstresses, specifically. And I need somebody small — small enough to get inside a loom and make repairs. A thin child would be ideal."

"I have no women at all." Vaskar said it without any particular regret. "I should have another shipment in a month or two. Sold every woman I had, a short while back. Creepy group."

Ysabel paused on the word for exactly the right length of time.

"Creepy?"

"Mm. Men in dirty hooded robes. Skin all over scars, what you could see of it. Nasty business." He shrugged. "They never said why they wanted all those women and I never asked. But I charged a premium, so — a good day's business."

"You are a gentleman, as ever, Vaskar. And a child? Have you any?"

"No. Don't get many children here. Haven't got one." He was already turning away. "I need to get back to work. My guards will show you out."

"No — wait." She had him by half a step. "Some of the stock you do have may have worked as tailors. I want to inspect them."

"Fine. Whatever." And then, with a smile that was not at all stupid: "And if you're trying to get a look at the Juggernaut, you don't need to sneak about. He's in the Deeps. My guard will take you round the property."

---

It was Virgil who noticed it, in the moment before they were ushered back down the stairs, from the one vantage point in that compound high enough to see over the wall.

The ground outside Bramblefen was exactly as dead as the ground inside — mud, and the occasional stand of weeds going brown with the season.

Except in one place.

Sixty yards out, past the midden line, there was a circle burnt into the ground, about twice as wide as a man is tall. The char had gone grey at the edges with age and nothing had grown back over it.

Nothing except the middle. Out of the ash, in a rough oval, something was growing — thick and waxy and very green, the only healthy green thing anywhere in that valley, in October, in a marsh.

He had no time to ask about it. The guard moved them down the stairs and out the front door, and on the way through the hall they all looked, because you could not help looking, at the shape under the canvas that was still wheezing.

---

The guard took them to the smaller prison first.

It was low security, which at Bramblefen meant eight cells with wrought iron doors and a single long lever on the wall that threw all eight at once. Beside the lever hung a ring with a great many keys on it.

Wolfram looked at the ring for slightly longer than he should have.

Ysabel went along the row stopping at each cell, asking about trades, playing the part exactly. Which gave JonLief his cover.

He crouched at the bars of a cell with an elf chained to the wall inside it and spoke to him in Elvish, which is a thing nobody in that building had done in a long time.

"Greetings, friend. I am sorry you are held here. If I can, I will try to help you. I am looking for a half-elf woman and a young boy. I am told they were here a couple of months ago. Do you know of whom I speak?"

"Sorry. I don't." The elf's voice had almost nothing left in it. "I've been here three months, but we don't get to socialise much, if you take my meaning. I've hardly been out of this cell." A pause. "I would be grateful for anything you could do."

JonLief tried the same with a half-elf two cells along, who spoke Elvish badly enough that they changed to Common, and who knew no more than the first.

When Ysabel had finished with all of them, the guard walked the party across the yard to the Deeps.

---

Inside the Deeps was a long hall with cells down one side, iron bars, locked.

At the end of the hall stood two large wooden doors with a small window set into them, and in front of the doors stood two guards who did not look like any of the others in that compound. They were not drunk. They were not enjoying themselves. They stood the way men stand when standing is what they have been paid for and they are good at it.

These would be Ironwake's, sent down with the champion.

Ysabel began her inspection as before, and JonLief went along the row looking for elf blood.

The first two conversations gave him nothing. The third gave him everything.

"Yes. I know who you mean." The slave came up to the bars to say it. "They were in the cell beside me. Right there, between mine and Old Cobb's. She was very sick. Just lay in there all day, not moving much. The boy kept at her to eat and she didn't seem to care about anything at all." He thought about it. "After a few days some guards came and took her away. Never saw her again. Same day, they came back and took the boy up into the tower. I never saw them bring him down, but I suppose they must have."

"When was this?" JonLief had both hands on the bars. "Have you heard anything more?"

"I'm sorry. I don't know exactly when. The days and the weeks bleed together in here." He shrugged, apologetic about it. "It was after all the women were bought, and before the Tabaxi got out. That's all I've got."

---

Luthien had spent the walk doing what Luthien does, which was wandering, looking at things, and trying out jokes on an audience of chained men who did not laugh at any of them.

At the end of the hall he reached the last cell — the one the elf had said was Old Cobb's — and looked in.

There was a very small man in there wearing an iron mask.

He had a coat on, except his arms were inside it, and the sleeves had been wrapped around his body and tied together behind him. The mask had no eye holes and no mouth.

"Who's this?"

A guard came over, pleased to be asked, and explained that this was the one going in against the Juggernaut at sundown. The whole camp was looking forward to it. The betting was on how many pieces the little man would come apart into.

Luthien saw his moment and turned on everything he had. He talked gambling with the guard — the finer points, how to get better terms out of a man who is too excited to think — and the guard warmed to him completely, and then Luthien steered it, gently, round to an elf woman and a child.

"Oh, aye. I remember them. Don't get many kids." The guard scratched his neck. "Woman was proper sick. When we saw green patches coming up on her skin, Vaskar had us take her out back and burn her. Don't want the Green spreading in here."

Luthien kept his face exactly where it was.

"The Green?"

"You've not heard of it? There's a plague out east, so they say. People start sprouting leaves and then they die." He grimaced. "Don't want that here."

"And the boy?"

"Children are worth money, and he looked healthy enough. Vaskar had us shut him up in the tower a week to be sure he hadn't got it. Once we knew he was clean, Vaskar sold him on." He jerked his head vaguely towards the house. "You'd have to ask Vaskar for the particulars. Vaskar, or Old Cobb — though I shouldn't think Old Cobb's going to live through the beating Vaskar's giving him."

Luthien's next question came out perfectly level.

"Why is Vaskar beating Old Cobb?"

"Couldn't say. Been at it all day, though."

---

"What's in the room at the end? Behind the two serious-looking gentlemen?"

"The Juggernaut." The guard was delighted. "You want to see? Go on. Have a look."

Luthien went to the door and put his eye to the little window.

It was dark inside. The only light in that room was what came through the window he was looking through, which meant he could see very little, and what he could see did not make sense. There was a boulder in the far corner. The room did not have a boulder in it.

Then the boulder moved, and it was a man's back.

He was, as Ysabel had said, easily the size of four large men. He was turned to the wall, and he was making small sounds, and after a few seconds of listening Luthien worked out what the sounds were.

He was playing.

He had a doll. It was made out of scraps of straw tied together, and he was walking it along the floor in front of him and talking to it.

Luthien, for reasons that made sense to Luthien, made a noise at him. Baby talk — the sort of thing you say to a dog you have decided is stupid.

The Juggernaut heard it, and flinched, and shrank back into his corner.

And a dwarf came out of the dark at the door like something fired from a sling.

"Leave him alone!" He hit the window with the flat of his hand hard enough to rattle it in the frame. "He's never hurt anybody!"

Then the giant came too. Up off the floor and forward with a bellow that came through two inches of oak and filled the hall, and Luthien went backwards off his feet entirely.

In the last half-second before he lost the window, he saw the thing properly: a full helm of iron, covering the whole face, studded all over with spikes.

The guard he had been charming was bent double laughing.

With that, the party left the Deeps.

---

Outside, the ring was finished. The sun was very nearly down.

On the way back across the yard Wolfram stopped at the blacksmith's and gave the armory door a proper look. The chain was heavy and the padlock was newer than anything else in that compound, and he had no way of picking it and said so. The door itself was another matter: old, and damp at the bottom, and he thought he could put his shoulder through it if it came to that.

Luthien did not like the noise a broken door would make, and started thinking about ways to conjure a key instead.

They had already worked out that the key to the armory was almost certainly on the ring hanging beside the lever in the smaller prison. They had decided against taking it. A missing ring of keys gets noticed, and being caught with it would have cost them more than the weapons were worth.

---

"We still have time before the match," said JonLief. "I want to see the place where my wife was burned. Virgil — come with me, please."

The two of them went out through the gate and across sixty yards of marsh grass to the circle.

Virgil crouched at the edge of it and then in the middle of it, and turned the leaves over, and found them strange in ways he did not have the time or the instruments to pin down. He took a cutting and a sample. Then he began to dig, carefully, to get at the roots and see what they were anchored in.

What they were anchored in was Elizabeth.

JonLief went down on his knees in the ash and did not say anything for a while. When he did, it was not to Virgil. He prayed, silently, and then he made a promise about finding out the truth, and then he got up.

They walked back to the yard without talking.

---

And Vaskar came out of his house carrying the body of an old man.

He carried it down the steps the way you carry a rolled rug, and he threw it out into the mud of the yard, and he said, loudly enough to reach the walls:

"This is what happens to people who betray me."

The old man was quite plainly dead.

"Poor Old Cobb," said one of the guards, to nobody.

---

The yard filled.

They brought out every slave in the compound to watch, because an audience was part of the entertainment, and the guards came off every post that could be left, and the whole of Bramblefen packed itself in around a circle of sharpened timber in the dark.

They walked the prisoner out first, still masked, still tied into the coat.

Then came the Juggernaut, and the dwarf beside him, and the difference in size between the champion and his opponent was not a thing the mind could take in all at once. The masked man came up to somewhere around the giant's thigh.

They took the mask off.

It was a gnome. Thin, blinking in the torchlight, and not afraid.

Then they cut the sleeves loose and pulled the coat away from him, and on the meat of his shoulder, in the pink and white of an old burn, was a sun with a tear falling from it.

The same one all four of them were wearing under their shirts.


Part Three — The Night the Fen Fell

They decided to wait.

It was not a brave decision and nobody pretended otherwise. The armory door would come off its hinges if Wolfram asked it to, and the sound of that happening would carry across the yard like a dropped bell. So they stood where they had been put, at the edge of a crowd of men who had been drinking since the fence went up, and they waited for somebody to start a killing.

Old Cobb was still lying in the mud by the Hall steps. Nobody had moved him. Nobody was going to.

Vaskar raised his voice and called for the fight to begin.

---

Luthien spoke first, and what he spoke was not a word anyone in that yard understood.

It was a small working, and a strange one to spend in the circumstances — not a weapon, not a wall. A kind of turning-aside. He put it on the gnome, and the air around the little man went briefly thick, the way air goes over a hot road, and then it did not look like anything at all.

The Juggernaut came off his mark like a landslide.

What happened next, the whole compound saw, and not one man in it understood.

He covered half the distance to the gnome and then stopped. Stopped the way a cart stops when a wheel comes off — all the momentum still in him and nowhere for it to go. He stood in the middle of the ring with his hands open and his masked head turning, and the thing he had been told to kill was eighteen inches from his knee, and he could not make himself want it.

So he wanted something else instead.

Mott bent, took hold of one of the barricade sections — a slab of lashed, sharpened logs as long as a man is tall — and tore it out of the ground like a weed. Then he turned, and threw it.

It went into the knot of men standing outside the doors of the Deeps: Ironwake's own, the two who had walked him out, the professionals. It caught both of them across the chest and carried them back into the stone and left them sitting in the mud, bleeding, wearing the astonished expression of men to whom the evening had just been explained.

There was a hole in the ring where the barricade had been.

Gimble was through it before the first man shouted.

---

Everything happened in the same breath, and the order of it only exists because somebody wrote it down afterward.

JonLief threw a shimmer of light into the air in front of Durgan — a cheap trick, a flicker to pull a man's eyes the wrong way. The dwarf did not so much as blink. He had spent nineteen years watching one thing and he was watching it now.

And Wolfram ran at the armory door and put his shoulder through it.

It did not creak and it did not splinter in the middle. It came off its hinges whole, in one piece, and went flat on the floor inside with a crash that ended every conversation in that compound.

The slaves understood it instantly. Nobody told them. Nobody had to. Thirty-odd people who had spent the evening being beaten for the entertainment of drunks turned, as one animal, and ran for the open door of the armory.

---

The guards nearest them tried to restore order the only way they knew.

A club came down on the back of a man's skull and he went into the mud and did not get up. Vaskar came forward off the Hall steps with two of his own behind him, shouting — *the armory, get them off the armory* — and for about four seconds it looked as though twenty armed men might still own that yard.

Then Durgan Half-Ear made the only decision of his life that mattered.

He had been handling that giant for nineteen years. He had kept him fed and kept him calm and kept him from doing exactly this, every day, in every town Ironwake sent them to, and he had done it because the alternative was a man he loved being put down like a dog. And he looked at a yard where every guard in the place had its back turned, and a hole in the fence, and a road north, and he understood that there would not be another night like this one.

He said: *Mott. Kill the guards.*

The giant reached over the barricade and picked two men up off the ground, one in each hand, the way a child lifts cats. Then he brought them down on the spikes of the fence he had built that afternoon.

---

Anselm Vogt reached the armory with three others and started handing weapons out through the door.

Wolfram stood in front of it and did not let anyone past. A guard came at him and he opened the man's arm to the bone, and the guard reeled off into the crowd, and then there was no crowd to reel into, because the crowd had turned.

This is the part nobody in Solteres will ever believe, and it is also what actually happened: they were mostly still unarmed. Thirty people with bare hands and fence timber against men in chain shirts with spears, and it was not close. One of them went onto a spearpoint and was carried along on it, and the man holding the spear was pulled down by six others and did not come back up.

Virgil raised his hand and brought a column of pale light down out of nothing onto a cluster of guards by the pens, and they staggered inside it, and he walked it slowly across the yard like a man moving a lantern, and wherever it went men fell out of the fight.

JonLief tried a working on a guard and missed him entirely. He made a small disgusted sound, drew his sword instead, and started around the outside of the ring to get at the men on the far side, which was the sort of thing he had been doing all chapter.

Mott took hold of another one and pulled his head off.

---

Up on the balcony of the Deeps — the good seats, the place you watched the fight from if you mattered — a dozen guards came off the rail at once and went for the stairs.

It would take them half a minute. Three flights, a landing, and the front door. Half a minute is a very long time in a yard like that one.

Wolfram cut the wounded guard's head from his shoulders, and the swing carried him round, and he put the edge of his shield into the face of the next man on the follow-through. The slaves, some of them armed now, closed over the last of the guards by the armory and killed every one of them.

Which was when Vaskar decided the night was lost.

He crossed the yard at a run, took Lady Ysabel Rochefort by the arm, and dragged her into his house.

---

Mott tore up another section of the fence and brought it down and hit nothing at all. Then he went down onto his knees in the mud with his chest heaving, and he stayed there, because he was finished. Four minutes of that will finish anything.

The balcony guards came out of the front door of the Deeps and found him kneeling.

They surrounded him and began to beat him. A dozen men with clubs and spear-butts on a thing that could not stand up, and they were not in a hurry, because he had just killed five of them and they wanted him to know about it.

Durgan threw himself on top of him.

He did not fight. There was nothing in him to fight with — a stout dwarf in a leather apron on top of a mountain, with his arms spread over as much of it as he could cover, taking every blow that would otherwise have landed. He had spent nineteen years keeping that giant from being killed, and when it came to it this was the whole of the method, and he used it.

Gimble Beren was not in the yard for that.

He had gone the other way — to the small prison, where the ones who could not fight had been left locked in the dark to listen. He went down the row and opened every door, and the people who came out of those cells were old, or sick, or too weak to lift a club, and they came out anyway.

The first thing the gnome ever did as a free man was let somebody else out of a cell.

---

In the yard, the slaves began tearing down the barricade.

It was between them and the dwarf and the thing he was lying on, and it had been built that afternoon by men who were now dead, and it came apart faster than it had gone up. Wolfram got his hands into it. Luthien got his hands into it. Thirty people pulled a wall down with their fingers.

When it came down they went over the top of it and killed every guard still standing in Bramblefen.

By the time it was quiet there were two men alive in that compound who had worn Vaskar's colours at sundown. Vaskar, and one guard, both of them inside the Hall, and Ysabel in there with them.

---

Wolfram stood at the bottom of the steps with blood drying on his shield and shouted up at the door.

"Vaskar. If you want to live you need to come out with Ysabel. She had better be unharmed."

He came out. Of course he came out. He was never a man who was going to die on principle — he was a manager, and he had been doing arithmetic since the moment the armory door hit the floor, and he had arrived at the only number available to him.

He offered a trade, and he offered it the way he would have offered a price on a woman: plainly, without heat, as though they were both professionals and this was Tuesday. The lady, unhurt. And the book — the real one, the current one, the ledger that recorded what Bramblefen had actually been doing these last six weeks. It was hidden. He would tell them where.

And then the last guard alive at Bramblefen put his hand up.

"I know where it is," he said. "I'll gladly tell you."

Vaskar did not even look at him.

The negotiation went on a while after that, and Wolfram ran it, and when it was done Ysabel came down the steps on her own feet and the book changed hands and Vaskar was given the gate.

They talked about killing him anyway. It was said out loud, by more than one of them, and it was not a stupid thing to say — nineteen dead men in the mud and the man who had owned them walking out through the gate with a bag on his shoulder.

Wolfram would not have it. Not from anyone. A thing agreed was a thing agreed, and he had agreed it, and that was the end of the discussion.

On his way across the yard Vaskar said the only true thing he said all night. Ironwake would have him killed for this. For letting the fen fall, and for the slaves — not the men, the *slaves*, the loss on the books. He said it without self-pity, as a point of information, in the tone of a man reading out a figure.

Then he walked.

Luthien stopped him in the gateway and went through his bag. Rations, and a great deal of coin.

Luthien took all of it.

Wolfram reached into the pile, took out a small handful, and dropped it back in Vaskar's bag. Then he put a hand between the slaver's shoulder blades and pushed him out the gate.

The rest of the coin he distributed among thirty freed slaves, in the mud, by hand, while Luthien watched him do it with the expression of a man being disembowelled.

---

Afterward, there was food.

Somebody had the idea of raiding the kitchens and the idea was unanimously popular. Thirty people who had been fed on whatever Bramblefen fed them sat in the yard of the place that had owned them and ate its stores, and for a while the loudest sound in that compound was chewing.

The party went into the Deeps while they ate.

They had all felt it on the way in and none of them had said much about it — the thing that came off that building, the pressure of three centuries of people kept in it. It was less than St. Pyotr's. It was recognisably the same.

They found a barrel.

Standard cooperage, iron-hooped, pitched at the seams, heavier than it had any right to be. And when they opened it, it was blood — fresh, and a long way from any animal, and every one of them had seen it before. On a beach on Dol Island, going aboard a ship. In a stone circle in a chapel at St. Pyotr's with black fog coming off it.

Virgil took a sample. The rest of it ran out through the bottom of the barrel and into the floor of the Deeps and was gone.

Nobody in that building said the obvious thing out loud, which is that there had been more than one barrel on that beach, and that somebody had been shipping them for a long time, and that this one had come *here*.

---

The freed began to get restless, the way people do when the fighting is over and nobody has told them what happens next.

So Luthien played.

He had been doing this since the first night of this whole business, in worse rooms than this, to worse audiences. But something has been happening to his music for a while now, and the people closest to him have started to notice it, and so has he, and none of them have said it. Every time he plays he is better than he was the time before, by more than practice accounts for.

That night in that yard he played better than he has ever played in his life.

And while he played, at the edge of his vision, there was a woman.

She was glowing — faintly, the way a window glows at dusk from outside — and she had too many arms. Four of them. Then six. It was difficult to be certain, because every time he turned his head to look straight at her there was nothing there at all, only thirty exhausted people sitting in the mud listening to a song.

When the last note went, she went with it.

He has not worked out who she was. He will.

---

Then Anselm Vogt came and offered the party his service, and the service of every one of them, because almost none of them had anywhere to go.

That was the part nobody had planned for. Thirty people. No food, no carts to spare, no road that led anywhere good. The party said so, carefully, and started trying to find a kind way to say *we cannot feed you* —

— and Anselm stopped them, because he had heard where they were going.

Solteres.

They could not go to Solteres. Half of them were not full-blooded human, and in that city that is not a quality, it is a legal status, and thirty freed slaves walking up the road to Solteres would be back in a pen inside a week with the paperwork done properly. They had to go the other way. Away from the city, and fast.

So they were told to go to Ald's Star, and to say that Wolfram sent them.

---

Ysabel got her book.

She asked for both, naturally, and was told no, and took the one with reasonably good grace — Cobb's ledger, thirty-eight years of Bramblefen's business in a dead clerk's hand. She has a plan for it, and she did not trouble to hide it: there are names in that book attached to houses in Solteres that would pay a great deal not to see them read aloud, and houses like that can lean on a man like Ironwake, and a man like Ironwake can be persuaded to forget about a trial by combat.

She was in a good mood. She had come to Bramblefen to buy seamstresses, been taken hostage, and come out of it holding a weapon.

The party kept the working ledger. It has a name in it.

---

And then, when the eating was done and the night had gone properly cold and it genuinely seemed that the day was finished with them, the head of a hammer came through the front gate.

One blow. The timber went in like a biscuit. Then a boot, and the gate swung wide, and two men walked into the yard in long cloaks of deep violet.

A very large older man, and a thin young one beside him.

The big one introduced himself as Baltus. He said he was a Shepherd, and that he had been sent to find them, and nobody in that yard needed to be told what the colour meant.

They told him everything.

They had no particular reason to and there was every reason not to — nineteen dead men, a slaver let go, a compound taken, a noblewoman in their company and a stolen book in their pack. They told him anyway, and he listened to all of it, standing in the mud with silver chiming softly on his coat, and when they finished he looked around that yard at thirty living people eating stolen bread.

He was *pleased.* Genuinely, visibly, uncomplicatedly pleased. He has thirty-one small silver lambs sewn onto that cloak and not one mark anywhere on him for the ones he brought home, and he stood in Bramblefen and looked at a night that had gone well, and it was plain to everyone watching that this does not happen to him often.

They laid out the plan. Use the ledger. Find where the women were taken, or find where that barrel was going. But Rowan first.

Baltus agreed with all of it. And he told them something about themselves that nobody in the Church had ever bothered to tell them: that a Penitent has the latitude to act beyond his orders when the chance arises to serve the will of the Emperor.

They had been walking around for two and a half months assuming every step off the road was a crime.

---

Virgil said he was not going to Solteres.

He said it without apology, which is how he says everything. The cultists were a threat and he had some concern for the boy, and neither of those was the thing keeping him awake. The thing keeping him awake was growing out of a scorched circle in a marsh sixty yards outside that wall, with small green nodules in rows on the underside of its leaves, and he had a sample of it in his pack, and he had come to a conclusion about it.

The Green was going to be a pandemic. He was going to find out what caused it and he was going to find a cure.

He was going to Master Thaddeus.

Baltus agreed that this was the right thing to do.

He also made it fairly clear, in the mildest possible terms, that he thought this party was out of its depth and had not always chosen the way the Church's teaching would have chosen. So he was leaving his apprentice with them.

Then he went back out through the gate he had broken.

---

They bedded down inside the walls of Bramblefen, because it was the only shelter for miles and because nobody had the strength left to be squeamish about it.

Thirty freed people slept in the yard around the cold ashes of the kitchen fires. The bodies of nineteen guards lay where they had fallen, out past the ring, and would be there in the morning, and after that would be somebody's problem. Durgan and Mott were already gone — north, before it was light, toward a mountain range the dwarf remembered from a long time ago, in the hope of finding somewhere nobody would ever ask either of them to fight again.

In the Deeps beneath them, a barrel's worth of a god's blood was soaking slowly into three hundred years of stone.

Nobody had counted the barrels. Nobody had asked where the women went. The gnome had a name and had not yet told them the half of it, and a boy they had crossed a continent for was in a city two days off, in the hands of a man nobody had thought to worry about, because he had walked out of that gate alive with a handful of coin and a grievance.

For one night, none of that was true yet.

They had taken a slaver's holdfast with their hands, and thirty people were asleep in the open who had been property that afternoon, and the Church had told them, for the first time, that they were allowed.

It was the best night they had had since the forge.
